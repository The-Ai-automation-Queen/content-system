## Draft 1 — LinkedIn Text Post

**Hook adapted from:** Allie K. Miller (Dots / always-on assistant, Oct 8 2026)  
**Pillar:** AI confusion to AI confidence / data and security awareness  
**Audience:** CEOs and business owners

---

Your AI assistant read your emails before you did this morning.

It flagged a billing discrepancy.
It spotted a scheduling conflict your EA missed.
It noticed an unanswered client message from three days ago.

That is not a productivity win.
That is a question about who is actually running the situation.

OpenAI launched Dots this week.
An always-on AI layer that sits inside your calendar, your inbox, and your notifications.
It surfaces problems before you have seen them.

Most of the coverage is enthusiastic.
Here is what the coverage is not asking:

What data is Dots reading right now?
Who owns that conversation log?
What happens to the billing email it flagged — after it flagged it?

The business leaders adopting this fastest will have the biggest information advantage.
The ones who do not ask these questions first will also have the biggest liability exposure.

AI is not slowing down its access to your operational layer.
The only question is whether you gave it access on purpose or by default.

What is the one system in your business you would not want an AI reading unsupervised?
Drop it in the comments — the pattern in the answers is useful for everyone here.

## Draft 2 — LinkedIn Text Post

**Gap addressed:** The verification bottleneck — no creator translated Wes Roth's science finding for business  
**Audience:** Mid-market CEO, $5M–$100M company  
**Pillar:** Internal capability not consulting dependency / AI confusion to AI confidence

---

Your team approved the proposal.
The AI wrote it.
No one read it closely.

This is not a future risk.
This is the current pattern in most companies using AI tools today.

In science, researchers are now calling it the verification bottleneck.
AI produces mathematics papers faster than mathematicians can check the work.
OpenAI published 722 manuscripts from an unreleased model this week.
Experts cannot tell which parts are correct without months of review.

Your business has the same problem at a smaller scale.
It is still a problem.

The AI writes the client brief.
Someone clicks approve.
The brief goes out.
A mistake is inside it that no one caught — because no one expected to need to look.

The companies that get this right are not the ones using AI the most.
They are the ones who have decided, deliberately, which outputs require human review before they leave the building.

That decision does not come from a tool.
It comes from a policy your team has agreed on.

Do you have one?

If you run a team that uses AI to produce client-facing work, what is your review rule?
Tell me below — I will tell you what I see in the patterns.
