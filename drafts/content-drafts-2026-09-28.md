## Draft 1 — LinkedIn Text Post

**Angle:** Deskilling as strategy (adapted from Ethan Mollick hook)
**Audience:** CEOs and business owners
**Target length:** 150–250 words

---

The most effective executives are not adding AI to their calendars.

They are removing tasks from their own job descriptions.

Ethan Mollick — Wharton professor, 450,000 Substack readers — posted this week:
"I am deskilling myself with AI on so many annoying tasks I do not want or need to be skilled at."

56,000 views.
Hundreds of comments from people who felt immediate relief.

Because the AI conversation has been framed backwards.

We have spent two years telling leaders to learn more.
The smarter question is: which skills should you stop owning personally?

Here is the distinction worth making:

Skills that require your specific judgement → keep them.
Skills that exist only because no tool handled them until now → those belong to AI.

The founder who writes every first draft.
The executive who formats every board presentation.
The CEO who manually builds the weekly performance summary.

That is not expertise. That is repetition.

Repetition is exactly what AI removes — when the decision is made to let it.

Your organisation does not need you to be better at the tasks AI has already claimed.
It needs your judgement redirected toward the decisions only you can make.

Which skill are you still personally owning that AI should handle?
Tell me below — I read every comment.

## Draft 2 — LinkedIn Text Post

**Angle:** AI asymmetry for mid-market CEOs — gap topic none of the monitored creators addressed
**Audience:** Mid-market CEO ($5M–$100M company)
**Target length:** 150–250 words

---

The biggest companies in the world spent 2025 building internal AI capability.

Microsoft. Salesforce. Amazon.
Dedicated AI teams. Custom models. Private data infrastructure.

Nina Schick put it plainly this week:
"The biggest risk is not powerful AI. It is the asymmetry of powerful AI — between a few companies and everyone else."

That asymmetry is sitting inside your organisation right now.

Not because AI is expensive.
Not because your team is not capable.
Because nobody has answered the question your leadership team is not yet asking:

Which three processes in your business currently require human time that AI could run reliably — starting this week?

Enterprise organisations hire someone internally to answer that.
Mid-market companies attend another webinar instead.

The organisations ahead in 2026 are not the ones with the biggest budgets.
They are the ones that made a decision about internal capability.

That decision does not start with a vendor.
It starts with a clear picture of where your team is still doing manually what AI is ready to own.

You do not need a consultant to map that for you.
You need an honest conversation inside your own leadership team.

Which process in your business would you hand off first?
Drop your answer in the comments.
