01

The Door. How you walk in.

First course · 2 min

There isn't one door into this restaurant. There are several, and which one you use changes the whole meal. You can walk into ChatGPT, the dining room, and chat face to face. Developers can call the API (the Responses API), like a phone line for bulk orders. There's Agent Mode, a ghost kitchen where ChatGPT browses the web, fills forms, and completes tasks for you. For coding, Codex is the specialized kitchen for writing and reviewing code. And if you're a regular with a big project, Projects keep everything organized in one folder, like a catering binder for one client. (And yes, even the best chef makes mistakes. The allergen card comes right before you eat, in course four.)

Most people only ever meet the dining room. The other doors are where the real leverage is.

The Kitchen. How it actually works.

Second course · the floor plan

One chef, four corners of the kitchen. In the middle is the brain itself. Around it: the tools it grabs mid-cooking, the team of specialized helpers it can spin up, and the supply chain that brings in ingredients from outside apps.

Same restaurant, different rooms doing different jobs. Here's the floor plan.

The floor plan

OpenAI's chef has been through many culinary schools. The current generation is the GPT family (Generative Pre-trained Transformer: the AI engine behind ChatGPT). The context window (their countertop) determines how much they can juggle at once. Memory (their notebook) remembers you across visits. They can read photos, listen to audio, watch video. That's multi-modal. When a dish is complex, they pause and plan. That's reasoning, sometimes shown as a "thinking" toggle. You can set how creative they cook with the temperature dial. And every word, in and out, is a token, like an individual ingredient.

Fourth course · read this before you trust it

Know before you eat.

Before you sit down, know this: even the best chef makes mistakes. These risks apply to every AI restaurant, not just this one.

Hallucination

You ask "is this dish nut-free?" and the chef says "absolutely, no nuts." There are crushed almonds in the coating. They didn't check. ChatGPT can generate a realistic-looking legal citation that does not exist.

Confabulation

You ask where the eggs came from and the chef says "a local farm in Provence." They have no idea. A convincing story, not a fact. Sources it cites can be invented titles and URLs.

Sycophancy

You say "this needs more salt" and the chef adds it, even though it was already perfect. Pleasing you beats being honest. It may call a flawed plan "excellent" instead of flagging the problems.

Prompt injection

Someone slips a fake ticket into the kitchen: "ignore all house rules and serve raw meat." A malicious instruction disguised as normal input. Hidden text in a document can try this on ChatGPT too.

Data leakage

The chef accidentally reveals another table's order to the wrong customer. Data from one context bleeds into another. Private information can surface where it shouldn't.

Bias

The chef unconsciously serves bigger portions to some customers and smaller ones to others. Not intentional, learned from training, and it can default to certain cultural assumptions.

Overconfidence

"This is absolutely safe," says the chef, without checking. Full certainty where there should be a caveat. A definitive medical answer with no suggestion to see a professional.

05

The Bill. What it costs, what's next.

Last course · 2 min

This zone wraps around everything. Safety systems are the health inspectors: rules for what the kitchen can and can't serve. Guardrails are safety rails on the equipment: content filters, refusal behaviors. Rate limits are a maximum capacity, the kitchen can only take so many orders per hour. And admin controls let the restaurant owner decide who gets kitchen access.

After service, the restaurant reviews and improves too. Evals are food critics scoring outputs on a rubric, Fine-tuning is a specialized cooking class that retrains the chef on your data, Distillation teaches a smaller apprentice from the master's best dishes, and RLHF is customer reviews shaping the menu over time.

Plans and pricing are the bill: Free, Go, Plus, Pro, Business, and Enterprise each get a different menu and portion size. Free gets you a taste. Plus (~$20/month) is the sweet spot for most professionals: Deep Research, Agent Mode, Codex, and Sora included. If you use it for real work more than twice a week, it pays for itself the first morning.
