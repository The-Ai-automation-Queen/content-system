The problem is coordination, not effort

You already work long hours and you still feel behind. That morning scramble, the one where you spend forty minutes working out what today even is before you do a minute of real work, isn't a productivity problem. It's a coordination problem.

And coordination is the one thing you can genuinely hand to a machine. Not the judgement. Not the relationships. The pulling together of what happened, what is due, and what needs deciding.

Seven days, about thirty to sixty minutes a day, and you'll have five scheduled workflows running without you.

What this will and won't do

It will send you a briefing before you open your laptop, close each day with a review that sets up tomorrow, turn your saved reading into drafts, and pre-write your weekly client updates.

It won't publish anything for you, reply to clients on your behalf, or replace your judgement on strategy, pricing or hiring. The point isn't to remove you from the business. It's to remove you from the coordination.

One rule before you connect anything

You're about to give an assistant a window into your inbox, your calendar and your client notes. That's real access, and it deserves one deliberate habit rather than a vague intention to be careful.

I call it the traffic light rule and I teach it in everything I make.

LightWhat it coversWhat you do
GreenAnything already public, or that you would happily read aloud in a room of strangers.Connect it. No thought required.
AmberInternal but not sensitive: your own notes, drafts, process documents.Connect it, after you have removed names and numbers you don't need it to see.
RedClient identities, contracts, financial detail, health, anything covered by an agreement.Don't connect it. Redact before it goes anywhere near this system.

Do this

Apply the traffic light before every connection you make this week, not after. It takes ten seconds and it's the difference between a system you can defend and one you'll quietly stop using because it makes you uneasy.

Machines are good at coordination and bad at consequences. Give them the first and keep the second.

The three things that decide whether this lasts

  • It has to arrive without you. If you have to open something to get the briefing, you'll stop within a fortnight. Push, don't pull.
  • It has to be short. A briefing you skim is a briefing you eventually ignore. Six lines. If it's longer, tighten the instructions, not your attention span.
  • It has to fail loudly. When a step breaks, you need to know that day. A system that silently stops is worse than no system, because you carry on trusting it.

Where this won't save you

Let me be straight, because this is where the honest version differs from the pitch.

It won't fix a business with no offer, no pipeline or no pricing. Coordination on top of an unclear business gives you a very well-organised account of a problem you have not solved.

It won't make you decide faster. It puts the decision in front of you sooner, which is genuinely valuable and isn't the same thing.

And it won't survive being ignored. Two weeks of not reading the briefing and it becomes another notification. Build the two that matter, read them daily, and add more only when the first two have become a habit you would miss.
