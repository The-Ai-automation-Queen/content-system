MORNING 12 min read
Free guide · The Freedom Business · 7-day playbook

Run a 24/7 company without working 24/7.

You already work long hours and you still feel behind. That morning scramble is not a productivity problem, it is a coordination problem. And coordination is the one thing you can hand to a machine. In 7 days, about 30 to 60 minutes a day, you will have five quiet workflows that run while you sleep. No code. No team. Works with Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, or Copilot.

What this system will and will not do

It will send you a morning briefing before you open your laptop, close each day with a review that preps tomorrow, turn your saved reading into content drafts, and pre-write your weekly client updates.

It will not publish anything for you, reply to clients on your behalf, or replace your judgment on strategy, pricing, or hiring. The point is not to remove you from the business. The point is to remove you from the coordination.

One rule before you connect anything: the Traffic Light Rule

You are about to give an assistant a window into your inbox, your calendar, and your client notes. That is real access. One habit keeps you safe, and I teach it in everything I make:

  1. 🟢 Green: your own drafts, public info, subject lines, your own calendar. Safe to hand over freely.
  2. 🟠 Amber: internal notes, emails from known contacts, half-finished plans. Fine to process, but strip names and numbers you would not want leaving your control. When in doubt, redact first.
  3. 🔴 Red: passwords, contracts, NDA material, client personal data, payment details. This never goes into a personal AI account. Ever. If a workflow would touch red-level material, it skips it and flags it for you.

Green flows, amber gets redacted, red stays out. Every prompt below is built to respect that.

Day 1. Set up your assistant and connect the essentials

This is the day most guides skip. If you have never done any of this before, go slowly and follow it step by step. It is the foundation for everything else, and it is easier than it looks. Give yourself the full hour today. The other days are quicker.

Step 1: Get the right subscription

The free version of these tools cannot run on a schedule or connect to your inbox. You need a paid plan. Create an account, then pick the tier that includes scheduling and connectors. On Claude, go to claude.ai and subscribe to Pro or Max, Cowork comes with it. On ChatGPT, go to chatgpt.com and subscribe to Plus, Pro, or Team to unlock Tasks and Connectors. On Gemini, get Gemini Advanced (Google One AI Premium) or use Gemini inside your Google Workspace. On Copilot, you need a Microsoft 365 Copilot license on your Microsoft 365 account. You only need one. Do not sign up for all four.

Step 2: Open the feature that runs on a schedule

This is the engine of the whole system, and each platform hides it in a slightly different place. On Claude, open Cowork. On ChatGPT, open Tasks. On Gemini, open Scheduled Actions in Workspace, or set up a Gem. On Copilot, open Copilot Studio and create a new agent. Menus move around, so if the exact word is not there, look for anything that says schedule, task, automation, or agent. That is the one.

Step 3: Connect your first account (the part that feels scary but is not)

A connector is simply permission for your assistant to read something you already own, like your inbox. Setting one up is the same every time:

  1. Find the connectors area. Look for Settings, then Connectors, Tools, Integrations, or Add apps.
  2. Click Connect next to the one you want. Start with your email.
  3. A window from Google or Microsoft opens. Sign in to your own account.
  4. It shows you exactly what the assistant will be allowed to do. Read it. This is where the Traffic Light Rule starts. Reading and drafting is fine. If something asks for permission to send or delete and you did not expect it, stop and check before you continue.
  5. Click Allow. The window closes and the connector shows as connected.

That is it. You just gave your assistant its first sense. If you ever want to undo it, the same screen has a Disconnect button. You stay in control the whole time.

Step 4: Connect only 3 things

Now connect your email inbox, your calendar, and your main cloud drive, and nothing else yet. Every connector is a new surface for something to go wrong, so you want the smallest possible starting point. You can always add more once the basics run without you.

Step 5: Write your system prompt

This is the short paragraph that tells the assistant who it is working for and how you want it to behave. Keep it plain, save it, and reuse it across every workflow. On Claude save it as a Project instruction, on ChatGPT as a Custom GPT or Project system prompt, on Gemini as a Gem, on Copilot as the agent's system message.

Your system prompt

You are the operations assistant for a solo founder. Your job is to reduce coordination overhead. Always summarise clearly. Flag anything urgent. Never send messages or take irreversible actions. Never store or repeat passwords, contracts, or client personal data. Always leave decisions to me. When in doubt, ask.

Day 1 done. You have a working assistant, 3 connectors, and a clear role. The hardest part is behind you.

Day 2. Build your morning briefing

This is the single workflow that changes your day the most. Schedule it for 07:00 in your timezone, and test it once by hand before you trust it.

Morning briefing prompt

Read my inbox from the last 24 hours. Read today's calendar. Summarise in 3 sections. Section 1: what needs my attention today, ranked by urgency. Section 2: meetings today with any prep notes I should remember. Section 3: anything I missed yesterday that still needs a reply. Keep it under 300 words. Do not include passwords, full account numbers, or other sensitive details. Write it to a doc titled Morning Briefing YYYY-MM-DD in my main folder.

Day 3. Build your end-of-day review

Same idea, different job. Schedule it for 18:00 or whenever you close your laptop. Between the briefing and the review you now have a continuous thread, so you never start a day from scratch again.

End-of-day review prompt

Read the doc titled Morning Briefing YYYY-MM-DD. Read the last 24 hours of email. Read the last 24 hours of calendar events. Summarise what got done, what got dropped, and what should carry into tomorrow. Write it to a doc titled Daily Review YYYY-MM-DD.

Day 4. Build your research and content workflow

Create a doc called Research Inbox. All week, paste in any link worth keeping. That is your only manual step. Then two scheduled runs do the rest: a Friday digest, and a Monday draft pass.

Friday digest prompt (14:00)

Read the doc titled Research Inbox. For each link, summarise the core insight in 3 sentences. Group insights by theme. At the bottom, propose 5 content ideas I could write about next week based on the themes. Save to a doc titled Weekly Research Digest YYYY-MM-DD. Do not empty the Research Inbox. I will archive it manually.

Monday draft prompt (09:00)

Read the latest Weekly Research Digest. Pick the 2 strongest content ideas. For each, write a 300 word first draft in a conversational tone. Save to a doc titled Content Drafts YYYY-MM-DD. Mark them as drafts. Do not publish.

Every Monday you open two drafts, edit, and publish yourself. The system does the boring 80 percent. You do the 20 percent that matters.

Day 5. Build your client and project weekly update

Keep one doc per client, where you drop notes through the week. Keep passwords, contracts, and personal data out of these docs. This is amber material, so redact anything you would not want leaving your control.

Weekly client update prompt (Sunday 17:00)

Read each doc in the folder titled Clients. For each client, write a short weekly update covering: what got done this week, what is blocked, what is next, and any questions I need answered. Save all updates to one doc titled Client Updates Ready to Send YYYY-MM-DD. Do not send anything. I will review and send myself.

Sunday evening you open one doc, review five pre-written updates, tweak the tone, and send them yourself in 20 minutes. Every client feels well managed.

Day 6. Make the system reliable

A system that runs five times a day but fails silently is worse than no system. Do four things. First, add a stop rule to every workflow: if any required doc is missing or empty, do not guess, write a short note to a doc titled System Log YYYY-MM-DD and stop. Second, create that System Log doc and skim it once a day. Third, set a 10-minute Sunday review to read the week of logs and fix any prompt that keeps failing. Fourth, test a break: disconnect your email, run the briefing by hand, and confirm it fails cleanly into the log instead of inventing an answer.

Day 7. Document and prepare to expand

Create a doc called My Operations System. Write down the platform you chose, your system prompt, the connectors you enabled, every workflow with its exact prompt and schedule, one paragraph on what the system is for, and a running note of what broke and how you fixed it. This is your recovery plan: if something goes wrong in three months, this doc rebuilds the whole thing in 20 minutes. Then pick one workflow to add next week. Not five. One.

The three things that decide whether this lasts

  1. Small scope. Add workflows one at a time. The systems that survive are the ones that grew slowly.
  2. Clear stop conditions. Every workflow needs to know when to hand back to you, or it drifts into noise you learn to ignore.
  3. Weekly review. Ten minutes every Sunday on the System Log. That is the entire maintenance cost.

Where this system will not save you

Scheduled assistants with connectors are powerful for the workflows above. They are not the right tool for multi-step content pipelines with strict brand voice, automated publishing to your social platforms, research pulling from dozens of sources with citations, or logic that chains several specialised assistants together. That is the line between a helpful assistant and a real operations team. The first one you just built. The second is a bigger, more advanced setup, and it is a separate playbook for a later day. Master these five workflows first.
