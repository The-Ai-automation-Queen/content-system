What a prompt actually is

A prompt is a brief. If you would not send it to a new hire, do not send it to a model.

  • Best forGetting a usable answer on the first try instead of the fifth.
  • Careful withOne-line questions. They get you one-line thinking.
  • What I would doSteal the four-part brief below and stop starting from scratch.

It is just what you type. That is the problem.

A prompt is the message you send. There is no more to the definition than that, and the simplicity is exactly why most people get poor results and conclude the tool is overrated.

Here is the thing I watch people do. They type "write me a marketing email", get back something bland and generic, and decide AI is not very good. But look at that request. If you handed it to a new person on their first morning with no other information, you would get something bland and generic from them too. The output was a fair reflection of the brief.

The gap between a disappointing answer and a genuinely useful one is almost never the tool. It is the forty seconds you did not spend saying what you actually wanted.

The four-part brief

Four things, every time. You will get them into muscle memory within a week and then you will do it without thinking.

  1. Role. Who should it be while it answers? "You are a bookkeeper explaining this to a client who is not good with numbers." This does more work than anything else on the list.
  2. Context. What does it need to know that it cannot guess? Your business, your customer, what has already been tried, what the constraint is.
  3. Task. The actual job, stated as one clear instruction. Not "help me with", but "write", "compare", "list", "rewrite".
  4. Format. What should come back? Length, structure, tone, whether you want bullet points or prose. If you do not say, you get its default, and its default is a wall of text.

Do this

Before you send a prompt that matters, read it back and ask: could a competent stranger do this job from what I have written? If no, you have found the missing piece.

The same request, three ways

Same job. Watch what changes.

What you typeWhat comes back
Thin. "Write a follow-up email." A generic template with square brackets in it. You rewrite the whole thing.
Better. "Write a follow-up email to a client who asked for a quote two weeks ago and has gone quiet." Something usable. Still sounds like everyone else. You rewrite half of it.
A proper brief. "You run a small design studio. A client asked for a quote a fortnight ago and has not replied. You have worked with them twice before and the relationship is good, so do not sound desperate or pushy. Write a short email, four sentences at most, plain language, no exclamation marks, that gives them an easy way to say the timing is wrong." Something you send with one word changed. Sounds like a person. Sounds like you.
The tool is rarely the weak link. The brief is.

Four habits that do most of the work

  • Show it one example of what good looks like. Paste in an email you were happy with and say "match this tone". This is worth more than any amount of describing.
  • Say what you do not want. "No exclamation marks, do not call it a journey, do not open with I hope this finds you well." Negative instructions work.
  • Ask for three versions. It costs the same, and picking beats editing.
  • Keep going. The first answer is a starting point. "Shorter. Warmer. Cut the second paragraph." Two rounds gets you most of the way.

Save the ones that work

This is the part almost everyone skips, and it is where the real time saving is.

When a prompt produces something good, keep it. A note on your phone is enough. Next time you need that job done, you paste the brief, change the details, and you are finished in ninety seconds instead of fifteen minutes.

After a month you will have eight or ten of these covering most of what you do repeatedly. That collection is worth more than any upgrade you could buy, and it is the point at which this stops being a novelty and starts being infrastructure.

Why this is worth learning once

Models change every few months. The people who write good briefs get better results from every one of them, and the people who type one line get disappointing results from every one of them. It is the one skill here that does not go out of date.
