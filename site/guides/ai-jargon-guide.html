The sentence that started this

Here is an actual sentence you might hear in a meeting, a podcast, or a LinkedIn post. Right now, it probably sounds like nonsense. By the end of this guide, you will understand every word of it.

Overheard in a meeting

“We’re connecting our customer service docs to a Claude agent through MCP, so it pulls from our own data instead of making things up. If quality slips, we’ll fine-tune. The context window should handle it. Tokens are cheap now.”

Seven technical terms in two sentences. No wonder everyone just nods.

Let’s break every one of them down. No jargon inside the jargon. Just plain language.

Group 1: The words in that sentence

We will start with the six terms hiding in that meeting sentence. Each one gets a plain-English definition, then a “think of it like” line so it sticks.

Hallucination

When AI confidently makes something up. It doesn’t know it’s wrong. It isn’t trying to lie. It just fills in gaps with things that sound right but aren’t. Think of it like a friend who never says “I don’t know.” They will always give you an answer, even when they’re guessing. That’s a hallucination. Why it matters: This is the single biggest reason you should always double-check AI answers on anything important. Especially numbers, legal claims, medical advice, or anything with real consequences.

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)

Instead of answering from memory (where hallucinations happen), the AI looks something up in your specific documents first, then answers based on what it found. Think of it like an open-book exam. The AI isn’t guessing from what it memorized in training. It’s reading your files and quoting from them. Example: A company feeds their return policy into an AI chatbot. When a customer asks “Can I return this after 60 days?” the bot reads the actual policy instead of making up an answer.

Agent

An AI that doesn’t just talk. It takes action. It can browse the web, read your files, send messages, fill forms, or run calculations. Regular chatbots wait for your question and give you text back. An agent goes and does things. Think of it like the difference between texting a smart friend for advice versus hiring that friend for the afternoon. They don’t just tell you what to do. They go do it. Example: You tell an agent “Find me flights to Lisbon under $400 for next weekend.” Instead of listing airlines you could check, it actually searches, compares prices, and comes back with options.

MCP (Model Context Protocol)

A standard way for AI tools to connect to other software. Like a universal adapter. Before MCP, every AI tool needed a custom connection to every app. MCP is one plug that works everywhere. Think of it like USB-C. Before USB-C, every phone brand had a different charging cable. MCP is the universal cable for AI apps. In that meeting sentence: “MCP connection to Zendesk” means the AI tool and Zendesk can talk to each other through this standard plug.

Context window

How much text the AI can “hold in its head” at one time. Every conversation has a limit. Once you go past it, the AI starts forgetting the earlier parts of your conversation. Bigger window means it can work with longer documents. Think of it like a desk. A small desk fits one page. A big desk fits a whole stack of papers. The AI can only look at what fits on its desk at once. Why it matters: If you are pasting a 50-page contract into a chatbot, you need a model with a big context window. Otherwise it literally will not see the end of the document.

Token

A small chunk of text. Roughly three-quarters of a word. AI companies charge by the token, the same way your phone plan charges by the gigabyte. 1,000 tokens is about 750 words. Think of it like syllables. The word “unbelievable” is one word but several tokens. Most short words are one token. Long words get split into pieces. Why it matters: When someone says “tokens are cheap now,” they mean the cost of using AI has dropped. That is actually true. Prices fell dramatically in 2024 and 2025.

You just decoded that entire meeting sentence. Read it again at the top. It makes sense now.

Group 2: Words you will hear this week

These six terms were not in that meeting sentence, but you will run into them soon. Same treatment: plain English, no jargon hiding inside the definition.

Fine-tune

Taking a general AI model and retraining it on your specific data so it behaves a certain way. It is expensive, slow, and usually overkill. Most people get better results from writing a good prompt or using RAG. Fine-tuning is the last resort, not the first step. Think of it like hiring a general contractor versus training someone from scratch. Most of the time, you just need to give clear instructions (a prompt). You only retrain when the instructions genuinely are not enough.

Temperature

A setting that controls how predictable or creative the AI is. Low temperature (close to 0) means it gives nearly the same answer every time. High temperature (close to 1) means it takes more creative risks. Think of it like a dimmer switch for randomness. Turn it down for facts. Turn it up for brainstorming. When does it matter? Honestly, almost never for most people. The default setting works fine for 95% of tasks. But now you know what it means when someone mentions it.

Multimodal

An AI that can work with more than just text. It can understand images, audio, sometimes video. Most major AI models are multimodal now. Think of it like a person who reads, looks at photos, and listens to voicemails, instead of one that only reads text messages. Example: You take a photo of a restaurant menu in Italian and paste it into the chat. The AI reads the image, translates the menu, and tells you what to order based on your dietary restrictions. That is multimodal.

Open source / open weights

The AI model is publicly available for anyone to download and run on their own computer. The big chatbots (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) are not open. They are owned by their companies. But other models (like Meta’s Llama) are open, meaning anyone can use them for free. Think of it like the difference between renting a commercial kitchen and owning your own stove. Both cook food. One gives you more control. Why it matters: If your business handles sensitive data and you do not want it passing through someone else’s servers, open models let you run everything in-house. For everyone else, the cloud tools work fine.

Prompt engineering

The skill of writing better instructions to get better results from AI. Not a programming language. Not a certification. Just learning how to be specific, clear, and structured when you ask the AI for something. Think of it like learning how to brief a freelancer. The better your brief, the better the work you get back. Same idea. Good news: We have a whole guide on this. It is the one before this one in the series, and it gives you a 4-part formula that works every time.

API (Application Programming Interface)

A way for two pieces of software to talk to each other behind the scenes. When someone says “we connected it through the API,” they mean the apps are exchanging data automatically, without anyone copying and pasting between them. Think of it like a waiter in a restaurant. You (the app) tell the waiter (the API) what you want. The waiter goes to the kitchen (the other app), brings back your order. You never go into the kitchen yourself. Example: Your accounting software pulls your bank transactions automatically every morning. That happens through an API. No human is downloading a CSV and uploading it somewhere.

Now read that sentence one more time

The same meeting sentence, decoded

“We’re connecting our customer service docs to a Claude agent through MCP, so it pulls from our own data instead of making things up. If quality slips, we’ll fine-tune.”

Translation:
  • The AI reads their actual support docs before answering customers (RAG)
  • It can take actions inside their help desk software, not just write responses (agent + MCP)
  • “Making things up” is the hallucination problem, and RAG is how they are solving it
  • If the answers still are not good enough, they will retrain the model on their specific data (fine-tune)
  • The AI can handle all their docs at once (context window) and the cost is reasonable (tokens)

You follow every word now. And the next time someone drops a sentence like that, you can ask the question nobody else will: “What happens when the AI can’t find the answer in your docs? Does it make something up, or does it say it doesn’t know?”

That question shows you understand the difference between RAG and hallucination. It moves the conversation forward instead of nodding through it.

These 12 words cover about 90% of the AI vocabulary in any business conversation. The other 10% you can look up the moment you hear them. But you will not need to look these ones up again.

The gap was never intelligence. It was just vocabulary. And you just closed it.
