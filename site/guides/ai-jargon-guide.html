The AI words they keep using

Most AI jargon is one ordinary idea wearing a lab coat. Here they are, in plain English.

  • Best forSitting in a meeting and knowing exactly what is being said.
  • Careful withNodding. Nodding is how you end up on the wrong project.
  • What I would doKeep this open in a tab for a fortnight. After that you will not need it.

The sentence this started with

Somebody said this to me, in a real meeting, with a straight face:

"We are running a RAG pipeline with a fine-tuned model, but the context window is limiting the agent, so we are looking at MCP to cut the token spend."

Everyone around the table nodded. I would guess two of them followed it. That sentence contains six pieces of jargon and one actual idea, and the actual idea is roughly "our thing is expensive and does not remember enough".

Here is every word in it, and six more you will meet this week. My translation job is to give you the plain version, not to make you sound clever.

The six words in that sentence

The wordWhat it actually meansWhy anyone cares
TokenA chunk of text, roughly three quarters of a word. How the tools count what you send and receive.It is how you are billed. Longer conversations cost more.
Context windowEverything it can hold in mind at once: your instructions, the conversation, the files.When a long session goes strange, this is usually why.
RAGRetrieval augmented generation. It looks something up in your documents before answering, rather than answering from memory.The standard way to make a tool that knows about your business.
Fine-tuningExtra training to make a model better at one narrow thing.Expensive, and usually unnecessary. A good brief solves most of what people fine-tune for.
AgentSoftware that takes multi-step actions towards a goal rather than answering once.Useful, and the word most likely to be stretched in a sales pitch.
MCPModel Context Protocol. An agreed standard for connecting AI tools to other software and data.It is why connecting things is getting easier and cheaper.

Do this

Next time a word goes past that you do not know, say so. "Can you put that in plain English?" You will find at least one other person in the room was waiting for someone to ask, and the person who cannot answer it has told you something useful.

Six more you will hear this week

The wordWhat it actually meansWhy anyone cares
HallucinationIt stated something false as though it were fact.The single most important failure to know about. It never warns you.
PromptWhat you type. The brief.The difference between a useless answer and a good one is almost always here.
System promptStanding instructions the builder set, which you do not see, shaping how it behaves.Explains why the same model behaves differently in two products.
MultimodalIt handles more than text: images, audio, sometimes video.You can photograph a document instead of typing it out.
Open weightsThe model file is published, so you can download and run it on your own hardware.The answer when data cannot leave your building.
GuardrailsRules and checks meant to stop it doing something unsafe or off-limits.Ask what they are before you put anything customer-facing on it.

Read that sentence one more time

"We are running a RAG pipeline with a fine-tuned model, but the context window is limiting the agent, so we are looking at MCP to cut the token spend."

In plain English: we built something that looks up our own documents before answering, we paid to train it on our data, it cannot hold enough in mind to do the multi-step job properly, and we are hoping a standard connector will bring the bill down.

That version invites the useful questions. Why did you fine-tune before trying a better brief? Which multi-step job, specifically? What is the bill? None of those questions are available while the jargon is doing its work.

What you walk away with

Not the ability to use these words. The ability to hear through them. Jargon in this field is not usually there to deceive you, it is there because the person speaking is repeating what they heard. But it does the same job as deception, because it stops you asking.

You do not need to be technical to make good decisions here. You need to be the person who says "in plain English, what does this do, what does it cost, and what happens when it is wrong?" That question works whether you understand the answer or not, and it is the only one that matters.
