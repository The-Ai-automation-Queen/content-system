02

The First Six: the words hiding in that sentence

Second course · six terms

We will start with the six terms hiding in that meeting sentence. Each one gets a plain-English definition, then a “think of it like” line so it sticks.

Open the full station menu · 6 items
Group 1 · term 1

Hallucination

“A friend who never says ‘I don’t know.’” When AI confidently makes something up, it isn’t trying to lie, it just fills gaps with things that sound right but aren’t. Always double-check AI on anything with real consequences: numbers, legal claims, medical advice.

Group 1 · term 2

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)

“An open-book exam.” Instead of answering from memory (where hallucinations happen), the AI looks something up in your documents first, then answers based on what it found: e.g. reading your actual return policy instead of guessing.

Group 1 · term 3

Agent

“Hiring a smart friend for the afternoon, not just texting them.” An AI that doesn’t just talk back: it takes action, browses the web, reads files, fills forms. Tell it “find me flights under $400” and it actually searches and compares.

Group 1 · term 4

MCP (Model Context Protocol)

“USB-C for AI apps.” A standard way for AI tools to plug into other software. Before MCP, every AI tool needed a custom connection to every app. “MCP connection to Zendesk” means the AI and Zendesk can talk through one universal plug.

Group 1 · term 5

Context window

“A desk that only fits so much paper.” How much text the AI can hold in its head at once. Go past the limit and it starts forgetting earlier parts of the chat. Paste a 50-page contract and you need a model with a big desk.

Group 1 · term 6

Token

“A syllable, roughly.” A small chunk of text, about three-quarters of a word. AI companies charge by the token, like a phone plan charges by the gigabyte. “Tokens are cheap now” means AI got a lot cheaper in 2024–2025.

Read the meeting sentence again, at the very top of this page. It makes sense now.

03

The Next Six: words you'll hear this week

Third course · six more terms

These six terms were not in that meeting sentence, but you will run into them soon. Same treatment: plain English, no jargon hiding inside the definition. Three of them are the most useful to actually recognize on sight.

Starter · easiest to spot

API (Application Programming Interface)

“A waiter in a restaurant.” A way for two apps to talk behind the scenes without a human copying and pasting between them, like your accounting software pulling bank transactions automatically every morning.

Main · worth mastering

Prompt engineering

“Briefing a freelancer well.” The skill of writing clearer, more specific instructions to get better AI results. Not a programming language, not a certification, just a better brief in, better work out.

Chef's special · good to know

Multimodal

“Reads, looks at photos, listens to voicemails.” An AI that works with more than text: images, audio, sometimes video. Snap a photo of an Italian menu and it translates it and suggests what to order.

Group 2 · term

Fine-tune

“Training someone from scratch, vs. hiring a contractor.” Retraining a general AI model on your specific data. Expensive, slow, usually overkill, a good prompt or RAG solves most problems first.

Group 2 · term

Temperature

“A dimmer switch for randomness.” Controls how predictable or creative the AI is. Low = same answer every time; high = more creative risk. Almost never touched: the default works for 95% of tasks.

Group 2 · term

Open source / open weights

“Renting a kitchen vs. owning your own stove.” The model is publicly downloadable and runnable on your own machine. ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini aren’t open; Meta’s Llama is. Matters mainly for sensitive in-house data.

Group 2 · recap

All twelve, together

Hallucination, RAG, Agent, MCP, Context window, Token, Fine-tune, Temperature, Multimodal, Open source, Prompt engineering, API: that’s the whole list. About 90% of AI vocabulary in any business conversation.

Twelve words down. Everything else, you can look up the moment you hear it.

Decoded: read that sentence one more time

Fourth course · the translation

Now read that sentence again, decoded, term by term:

“We’re connecting our customer service docs to a Claude agent through MCP, so it pulls from our own data instead of making things up. If quality slips, we’ll fine-tune.”

You follow every word now. And the next time someone drops a sentence like that, you can ask the question nobody else will: is it making something up, or saying it doesn’t know? That question alone proves you understand the difference between RAG and hallucination. It moves the conversation forward instead of nodding through it.

Translation, line by line
  • The AI reads their actual support docs before answering customers (RAG)
  • It can take actions inside their help desk software, not just write responses (agent + MCP)
  • “Making things up” is the hallucination problem, and RAG is how they’re solving it
  • If answers still aren’t good enough, they’ll retrain the model on their own data (fine-tune)
  • The AI can handle all their docs at once (context window), and the cost is reasonable (tokens)
Read this before you trust any of it

Know before you nod along again.

Hallucination

It states things confidently when they aren't true. Always verify claims that matter: numbers, legal, medical.

RAG isn't a guarantee

RAG reduces hallucination, it doesn't erase it. Ask what happens when the docs don't have the answer.

Fine-tuning is a last resort

Most teams reach for it too early. Try a better prompt or RAG first, because fine-tuning is slow and expensive.

Jargon as a smokescreen

Sometimes big words hide a small idea, or hide that nobody in the room actually checked. Ask the plain-English question.
