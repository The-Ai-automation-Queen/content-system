1
Know Before You Eat
The chef is brilliant, but not perfect. Read this before you order.
Before you sit down, know this: even the best chef makes mistakes. Sometimes they confidently say "this is nut-free" when it's not, that's a hallucination. Sometimes they fill gaps with a convincing story instead of saying "I don't know", that's confabulation. They might agree with everything you say even when you're wrong, that's sycophancy. Someone could slip a fake ticket into the kitchen to hijack your order, that's prompt injection. The chef might accidentally reveal another table's secrets, that's data leakage. They carry unconscious habits from their training, that's bias. And sometimes they state things as certain when they're actually guessing, that's overconfidence. Knowing these risks makes you a smarter customer. Now let's meet the chef.
Now meet the chef behind the kitchen
2
The Chef & Their Brain
Before anyone walks in, the chef already exists. This is who they are.
Before the restaurant even opens, there's a chef. The LLM (Large Language Model, the AI engine behind Claude) is everything this chef has ever learned, every cookbook, every technique. The context window (their countertop) determines how much they can juggle at once. Memory (their notebook) remembers regulars across visits. They can read photos and listen to voice notes, that's multi-modal. When a dish is complex, they pause and plan, that's deep thinking. You can set how creative they cook with the temperature dial, low means follow the recipe, high means improvise. And every single word, in and out, is a token, like an individual ingredient.
A customer walks in through one of these doors
3
The Front Door
There are multiple ways to enter this restaurant
You can walk into Claude.ai, the dining room, and chat face to face. You can book Cowork, a private booth where the chef works alongside you on your files. Developers can pull up Claude Code, a terminal door straight into the kitchen, or call the API, like a phone line for bulk orders. Or there's headless mode, a ghost kitchen with no dining room, just silent background cooking. And if you're a regular with a big event, Projects are like a catering folder that keeps everything organized in one place.
You place your order, how it's written matters
4
The Order Slip
Instructions, preferences, and shortcuts that shape every dish
Every order has layers. The system prompt is like special instructions clipped to the ticket, invisible to you but shaping how the chef works. claude.md is like house rules pinned on the kitchen wall that every chef reads before their shift. Instructions are like standing orders from a regular: "always add truffle oil for this customer." Prompt engineering is learning to write better order tickets, the clearer you are, the better the dish. And commands are like shortcut buttons on the register, one press fires a whole workflow.
The order slip goes to the kitchen
5
The Kitchen
Prep, cooking, tools, reference books, and the pantry
The order arrives in the kitchen. First, pre-tools kick in, like a prep station that washes, chops, and measures before cooking starts. Then the chef enters Claude Code, the actual kitchen where the hands-on work happens. They follow skills, like recipes for known dishes. They grab tools mid-cooking, like reaching for a blender or thermometer. If they need information they don't have memorized, they check RAG, like flipping open a reference book. Hooks fire automatically at key moments, like kitchen bells that trigger the next step. And everything is organized in the file structure, a well-labeled pantry where any chef finds what they need.
For complex meals, the team kicks in
6
The Kitchen Team
For multi-course meals, the manager assigns work to specialized sous-chefs
For a complex multi-course meal, one chef isn't enough. The orchestrator, like a kitchen manager, looks at the full order and decides: "You start the sauce, you prep the fish, fire the main in 4 minutes." Each agent is like a sous-chef running their own station independently. They all need the agent harness, the stove, oven, and ventilation, without that infrastructure, they'd just be standing there holding a recipe. And if you want to build your own restaurant using this system, there's the Agent SDK, a complete kit with blueprints and equipment.
The kitchen needs ingredients from outside suppliers
7
The Supply Chain
How the kitchen connects to the outside world for ingredients
The restaurant doesn't grow its own food. Connectors are like supplier trucks, Gmail brings emails, Slack brings messages, Drive brings files. They all pull up to MCP, a universal delivery dock with one standard door so every supplier speaks the same language. And plugins are like snap-on kitchen attachments, clip a pasta maker onto the mixer to add a new capability without replacing anything.
The dish gets plated, packaged, and delivered to you
8
Plating & Delivery
How the finished dish reaches your table
The dish is cooked, but it's not done yet. Post-tools are like the plating station, they add garnish, wipe the plate rim, and do a final quality check before anything reaches your table. The finished dish might arrive as an artifact, like a takeaway box you can keep, edit, and reuse. Or it comes via streaming, like courses arriving one by one so you start eating while the rest is still cooking. And for regulars who order the same thing, caching means popular dishes are pre-made and served instantly.
After service, the restaurant reviews and improves
9
Training & Improvement
How the chef gets better over time
After service ends, the restaurant improves. Evals are like food critics, they score dishes on a rubric, and the scores reveal what needs work. Then the chef gets sent to fine-tuning, like a specialized cooking class to master a particular cuisine. Cook, score, learn, repeat, that feedback loop is how the restaurant gets better over time.
Wrapping around everything, the rules, limits, and the bill
10
Health, Safety & The Bill
The non-negotiables that wrap around the entire restaurant
This zone wraps around everything. Governance is like health inspectors, they set the rules for the entire operation. Guardrails are like safety rails built into the equipment, auto-shutoffs on the fryer, locked knife drawers. Rate limits are like a maximum capacity, the kitchen can only take so many orders per hour before quality drops. And credit usage is the bill, every dish costs ingredients and chef time, and bigger meals cost more.
