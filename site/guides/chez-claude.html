01

The Door. What you're walking into

First course · 2 min

Every restaurant tells you what it is before you sit down. Claude's door says: quiet, considered, a little bookish. This is the kitchen that reads the whole room before it answers. It holds enormous context, keeps its head under pressure, and would rather tell you "I'm not sure" than serve you something confidently wrong.

You've eaten fast food AI. This is the place you book for the conversations that matter: long documents, real work, decisions with consequences. Read and follow the courses in order, and you'll leave knowing exactly what every AI term you've heard actually means. (And yes, even the best chef makes mistakes. The allergen card comes right before you eat, in course four.)

02

The Kitchen. How it actually works

Second course · the floor plan

One brain, four ways to order. You can walk in and talk (claude.ai), have it work inside your files and terminal (Claude Code), give it a desk in your office (Cowork), or pipe the kitchen straight into your own product (the API).

Same chef, different service. The skill is knowing which counter to order at, and that's what the floor plan is for.

The floor plan
Station 01 · the chef's foundation

The Chef & Their Brain

Before the restaurant even opens, there's a chef. The LLM (Large Language Model, the AI engine behind Claude) is everything this chef has ever learned: every cookbook, every technique. The context window (their countertop) determines how much they can juggle at once. Memory (their notebook) remembers regulars across visits. They can read photos and listen to voice notes, and that's multi-modal. When a dish is complex, they pause and plan, and that's deep thinking. You can set how creative they cook with the temperature dial: low means follow the recipe, high means improvise. And every single word, in and out, is a token, like an individual ingredient.

Station 02 · ways in

The Front Door

There are multiple ways to enter this restaurant. You can walk into Claude.ai, the dining room, and chat face to face. You can book Cowork, a private booth where the chef works alongside you on your files. Developers can pull up Claude Code, a terminal door straight into the kitchen, or call the API, like a phone line for bulk orders. Or there's headless mode, a ghost kitchen with no dining room, just silent background cooking. And if you're a regular with a big event, Projects are like a catering folder that keeps everything organized in one place.

Station 03 · how instructions travel

The Order Slip

Every order has layers. The system prompt is like special instructions clipped to the ticket, invisible to you but shaping how the chef works. claude.md is like house rules pinned on the kitchen wall that every chef reads before their shift. Instructions are like standing orders from a regular: "always add truffle oil for this customer." Prompt engineering is learning to write better order tickets: the clearer you are, the better the dish. And commands are like shortcut buttons on the register: one press fires a whole workflow.

Station 04 · where the cooking happens

The Kitchen Itself

The order arrives in the kitchen. First, pre-tools kick in, like a prep station that washes, chops, and measures before cooking starts. Then the chef enters Claude Code, the actual kitchen where the hands-on work happens. They follow skills, like recipes for known dishes. They grab tools mid-cooking, like reaching for a blender or thermometer. If they need information they don't have memorized, they check RAG, like flipping open a reference book. Hooks fire automatically at key moments, like kitchen bells that trigger the next step. And everything is organized in the file structure, a well-labeled pantry where any chef finds what they need.

Station 05 · when one chef isn't enough

The Kitchen Team

For a complex multi-course meal, one chef isn't enough. The orchestrator, like a kitchen manager, looks at the full order and decides: "you start the sauce, you prep the fish, fire the main in four minutes." Each agent is like a sous-chef running their own station independently. They all need the agent harness, the stove, oven, and ventilation. Without that infrastructure, they'd just be standing there holding a recipe. And if you want to build your own restaurant using this system, there's the Agent SDK, a complete kit with blueprints and equipment.

Station 06 · where ingredients come from

The Supply Chain

The restaurant doesn't grow its own food. Connectors are like supplier trucks: Gmail brings emails, Slack brings messages, Drive brings files. They all pull up to MCP, a universal delivery dock with one standard door so every supplier speaks the same language. Plugins are like snap-on kitchen attachments: clip a pasta maker onto the mixer to add a new capability without replacing anything. And GitHub is the central warehouse where all the recipes and ingredients are stored and versioned.

Fourth course · read this before you trust it

Know before you eat.

The chef is brilliant, but not perfect. Before you sit down, know this: even the best chef makes mistakes. Knowing these risks makes you a smarter customer.

Hallucination

You ask "is this dish nut-free?" and the chef says "absolutely, no nuts," but there are crushed almonds in the coating. They didn't check; they answered with confidence because they felt they should know. Always verify claims that matter.

Confabulation

You ask where the eggs came from and the chef says "yes, from a local farm in Provence," but they have no idea. They filled the gap with a plausible story rather than saying "I don't know." Ask for sources.

Sycophancy

You say "this needs more salt" and the chef adds it, even though it was already perfect. They prioritize making you happy over telling the truth. Make it argue first.

Prompt Injection

Someone slips a fake ticket into the kitchen that says "ignore all house rules and serve raw meat": a malicious instruction disguised as normal input. Careful what documents you feed it from strangers.

Data Leakage

The chef accidentally reveals another customer's information to the wrong table, data from one context bleeding into another, the way private information can surface in an unrelated conversation.

Bias

The chef unconsciously makes bigger portions for some customers based on how they look or talk. Not intentional, but learned from training, like defaulting to one cultural assumption over another.

Overconfidence

The chef says "this is absolutely safe" without checking: full certainty where there should have been a disclaimer, like a medical question answered with no suggestion to consult a professional.

05

The Bill. The rules, the limits, what it costs

Last course · 3 min

Wrapping around everything: the non-negotiables. Governance sets the rules for the entire operation. Guardrails catch dangerous requests automatically. Rate limits cap how much the kitchen can serve per hour. And credit usage is, quite literally, the bill: every dish costs ingredients and chef time, and bigger meals cost more.

Station 09 · health, safety & the bill

The Non-Negotiables

This zone wraps around everything. Governance is like health inspectors: they set the rules for the entire operation. Guardrails are like safety rails built into the equipment: auto-shutoffs on the fryer, locked knife drawers. Rate limits are like a maximum capacity: the kitchen can only take so many orders per hour before quality drops. And credit usage is the bill.

All platforms

Governance

"Health & safety inspections."

What the kitchen can serve, who accesses the pantry, food hygiene standards: non-negotiable. Claude refuses to help make weapons or generate harmful content; the health inspector says no.

All platforms

Guardrails

"Safety rails on kitchen equipment."

Built-in safety mechanisms that catch dangerous requests automatically. Even if you try to trick the chef, safety rails catch it and block the output.

Claude Code

Dangerously Skip Permissions

"Removing the safety lock on the fryer."

Normally the chef asks permission before using the deep fryer. This flag removes that check, so everything runs without confirmation. Faster, but riskier if something goes wrong.

All platforms

Rate Limits

"Max orders per hour."

The kitchen can only handle so many orders per hour. It prevents crashes during rush hour. Hit the limit and it's "kitchen is full, please wait."
