One summary a day: the inbox manager

Stop reading your inbox. Read one summary of it, once a day, and act on four lines.

  • Best forAnyone who opens their inbox to check one thing and loses an hour.
  • Careful withLetting it reply on your behalf before you trust its judgement.
  • What I would doSet it to summarise only. Add sending later, or never.

What you are building

An AI employee that reads, sorts and drafts your email so you only handle the things that genuinely need you. It works the same on Gmail and Outlook. It takes about an hour to build.

The tools are boring on purpose: your email, one automation tool such as n8n or Make, where the free tiers are fine, and one AI. Nothing here needs code.

If you spend an hour and a half a day on email, and most people I work with do, that is a serious slice of your year spent sorting other people's priorities into an order. This does the sorting. You do the deciding.

The safety rules. Read these first.

The rule that matters most, and I want it in front of you before you build anything: set the no-send rule at the connection level, not as a polite instruction in a prompt.

When you connect Gmail or Outlook, grant read, modify and draft permissions. Do not grant send. Now it cannot send an email, whatever happens, whatever it misunderstands, whatever is buried in a message it reads. An instruction can be overridden. A permission cannot.

Two more, briefly. Treat every incoming email as untrusted, because a message can contain text designed to redirect an automated reader. And never let it touch anything with a password reset, a payment or a legal deadline in it. Those go straight to you.

Do this

Before you connect anything, go to your email account's permissions screen and read what you are about to grant, in full. If send is on the list, take it off. That one screen is the whole safety story.

Part one. The filters. Ten minutes, no AI.

Do this even if you build nothing else, because it is where most of the win is and it takes ten minutes.

In Gmail go to Settings and then Filters, or in Outlook use Rules. Set up rules that move the obvious categories out of your main inbox before you ever see them: newsletters, receipts, notifications, calendar invitations, anything from a no-reply address.

Most people's inbox problem is not volume. It is that four genuinely important messages are hidden among sixty that are not. Sorting is most of the cure and it needs no intelligence at all.

Half of what feels like an email problem is a filing problem you have never sat down and solved.

Part two. The triage. Thirty minutes.

Now the AI part. Build one workflow that runs through yesterday's mail and puts each message into exactly one of four buckets.

BucketWhat goes in itWhat happens
Needs youA decision only you can make, a client, money, anything with a deadline.Stays in the inbox, flagged. You handle it.
Needs a reply, not a decisionScheduling, standard questions, things you answer the same way every time.A draft is written and left in your drafts folder.
For informationUpdates, threads you are copied into, things you should know about.One line in the daily summary. Archived.
NothingMarketing, notifications, noise the filters missed.Archived silently.

Write the bucket definitions in your own words, with two real examples from your own inbox for each. That is the job description, and it is what makes this work for your business rather than in general.

Part three. The daily summary. Fifteen minutes.

One message, at a fixed time, before you open your laptop. It should be short enough to read standing up.

  • What needs you today, with a one-line reason for each.
  • What has been drafted and is waiting in drafts.
  • Anything worth knowing but not doing.
  • Anything that has been sitting more than three days without a reply.

That last line is the one that quietly earns its keep. The email you meant to answer on Tuesday and have now half-forgotten is the one that costs you a client.

The upgrade path, and where to stop

Run it in summarise-and-draft mode for a month. Read the drafts before you send them and notice what it consistently gets wrong. Fix that in the job description, not in the individual email.

After a month you will be tempted to let it send the easy ones by itself. My honest advice: do not, unless the category is genuinely mechanical, like confirming a meeting time. The time saved by not pressing send is about four seconds. The cost of one wrong email to the wrong client is considerably more than that.

Draft-only is not a training-wheels stage you graduate from. For most small businesses it is the right permanent setting.
