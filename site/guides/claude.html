Tool verdict / Anthropic

Claude

Use it for anything that needs to be read carefully and thought through. Don't treat it as the final word.

  • Best forLong documents, complex drafts, work that has to hold its context, and code.
  • Careful withFacts, numbers, and anything going out under your name without a check.
  • What I would doStart free. Pay for Pro the week the limits start costing you real time.

Where it earns its keep

Claude is the one I reach for when the job is reading. Not skimming, reading. Give it three contracts, a policy document and last quarter's report and ask what changed between them, and it will hold all of that in its head at once and give you a straight comparison. Most tools lose the thread halfway through. This one doesn't.

The second thing it's genuinely good at is long work that develops over several rounds. A proposal you rewrite four times. A training document that has to stay consistent across forty pages. You can keep instructions, reference files and the running draft together in a Project, and it will still know on Thursday what you told it on Monday.

The third is code, through Claude Code, which works in a terminal rather than a chat window and can read a whole codebase, change files across it and run the tests. If nobody on your team writes code, ignore that entirely. If someone does, it's the strongest reason on this page to pay.

Where it'll burn you

It will state something wrong with complete confidence. Not often, but often enough that you cannot skip the check, and the confidence is the dangerous part. A hedge would warn you. There's no hedge. If a number, a date, a quote or a legal detail is going out into the world, open the source and look at it yourself.

It will also fill a gap rather than admit there's one. Ask about something it doesn't know and you can get a fluent, plausible, entirely invented explanation. The fix is cheap: ask it what it's unsure about and what it's assuming, before you ask it for the answer.

It agrees with you more than it should. If you frame a question as though you have already decided, it will help you build the case. That feels like a good assistant and it's actually a risk. Ask it for the strongest argument against your position before you commit to anything.

And it treats what it reads as information, not as instruction, only up to a point. A document or a webpage can contain text designed to redirect it. If you're pointing it at material you didn't write, treat that material as untrusted, the same way you would treat an attachment from a stranger.

Do this

Before you use anything it produced, ask one question: what would it cost me if this specific line were wrong? If the answer is nothing, ship it. If the answer is a client, a contract or your reputation, go and check that line yourself.

It can carry the work. It cannot carry the consequences.

What I'd actually do

Start on the free plan and give it a real job, not a test. Something you were going to do that afternoon anyway. Free covers chat, web search, files, code execution, connectors and extended thinking, with limits on how much you can use.

You'll know within a fortnight whether it fits how you work. The signal to pay isn't excitement, it's friction: you hit a usage limit in the middle of something and it costs you twenty minutes. That's when Pro is worth twenty dollars, and not before.

The five words worth knowing

You'll meet these in every conversation about Claude, and each one is an ordinary idea underneath.

  • Context window. Everything it can consider at once: your instructions, the conversation so far, and every file you attached. When work goes strange halfway through a long session, this is usually why.
  • Projects. A folder that keeps related chats, files and standing instructions together, so you stop re-explaining yourself every morning.
  • Artifacts. The document, chart or interface it builds beside the conversation, so you can look at the actual thing instead of a description of it.
  • Claude Code. The terminal version that reads a repository, edits files and runs tests. For developers. Genuinely good.
  • Connectors and MCP. Controlled access to your other tools and data. Useful, and the one place to be deliberate about what you switch on.

Is it the right one for you?

If most of your work is reading, comparing, drafting and revising, yes. It's the best of the current crop at holding a lot of material in mind and staying consistent across it.

If most of your work is quick answers, images and a bit of everything, ChatGPT is the better generalist. If your business already runs on Google Workspace, Gemini's access to your own calendar and files will beat Claude's raw ability most days. Pick for the shape of your week, not for the benchmark scores.
