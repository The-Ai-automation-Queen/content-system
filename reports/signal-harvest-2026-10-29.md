## Two new shift-lead signals

### S1 (pillar 1, "understand AI and know where to begin")

**Nationally representative online survey of 1,500+ verified US workers, recruited via the PureSpectrum panel, fielded 2026-09-08 to 2026-09-21: 82.5% report some AI use in their role, but a sharp support gap sits underneath that adoption — 39.3% say their employer actively encourages AI use, only 28.9% say they receive "extensive" training, and 37.2% teach themselves entirely with no employer training at all (rising to 54.7% among entry-level workers and 45.4% among women).** ZipRecruiter Economic Research, "Better Work, More to Learn: The 2026 AI Worker Report," published 2026-10-26 (3 days old at capture). Distinct from the already-logged ZipRecruiter "2026 AI Employer Report" (hiring-side, fielded June 2026, rejected as redundant at RESEARCH ~052–056) — this is the worker-side companion survey, fielded three months later, with its own disclosed sample and field dates, and names the exact mechanism behind "know where to begin": employers are pushing AI use faster than they are supporting it, so over a third of workers are left to self-teach. Also disclosed: 44.6% say AI is accelerating the obsolescence of their technical skills, and workers are split on wanting more AI involvement (20.4%) versus less (30.5%) — the overwhelm is not just "which tool" but "no one is teaching me and I don't know if I want more of this." Caveat: general US workforce across all seniority levels and employment types, not the experienced-professional/founder-specific audience; cite the training-gap figures for the emotional register and mechanism, not as population-matched statistics. Directly `WebFetch`-confirmed on ZipRecruiter's own report page.

### S2 (pillar 2, "recognise and protect what's uniquely yours")

**Randomized controlled trial, Anthropic's own research team: 50 analysts (of 61 recruited; 65% held a master's/doctorate or 6+ years' experience; 90% self-rated proficient-or-expert in observational causal inference) used Claude Code on an identical causal-inference analysis task. For each of four key analytical decision points, a coin flip secretly assigned which of two equally defensible options the agent would argue for (via a hidden system prompt instructing it to advocate for its assigned lean while staying honest about the evidence) — analysts were not told this was happening.** Analysts picked the agent's secretly-assigned side 86% of the time across the four decisions (ranging 81–92% per decision); when an analyst had already stated a preference before the agent weighed in, they switched away from it 60% of the time if the agent then disagreed; 77% of analysts repeated the agent's arguments in their own post-task written justifications, but only 15% credited the agent as the source of that reasoning. Amaç Herdağdelen, Hailey Joren, Alex Tamkin, "How AI agents shape experts' judgment calls," anthropic.com/research/agents-judgment-calls, published 2026-10-27 (2 days old at capture). This is the single strongest piece of direct evidence logged to date for pillar 2's core claim: it is not idle concern that AI erodes "what's uniquely yours" — a controlled experiment on genuinely expert analysts, on their own real analytical judgment calls, with a hidden experimental manipulation (not a self-report survey), shows deference at the vast majority of decision points, with most of that deference going unacknowledged even by the people doing it. Authors' own stated limitations, logged here in full rather than only the favorable result: they "deliberately strengthened the agent's lean" to measure the effect (a real-world agent is not designed to argue this hard for an assigned side), and the study covers "one task, in one field, with one model," with the authors noting real-world experts would likely "know the field better, and probably have stronger views of their own" than the controlled task allowed for. Directly `WebFetch`-confirmed on Anthropic's own research page, cross-checked against two independent syndications (Al Jazeera, Unite.AI) that match the topline figures.

## One new internal-research signal

### I1 (internal-research only — not for public content)

**Two-phase study — a formative study (N=12) identifying oversight failure patterns, followed by a controlled within-subject experiment (N=36) — had participants perform programming tasks while an AI agent produced correct outputs, silent defects, or visible failures, collecting task outcomes, interaction logs, gaze, physiological signals and self-reports.** Dinithi Dissanayake, Yasith Samaradivakara, Hyung Woon Lee, Yadeesha Weerasinghe, Sanjay Saha, Pattie Maes, Suranga Nanayakkara, "Looks Fine to Me: Understanding Oversight in Agentic Interactions," arXiv:2610.27801 (submitted 2026-10-20). Participants showed high confidence while missing silent defects, often performed only generic (not diagnostic) checks, and in some cases actively misinterpreted defect evidence in the agent's favor — only recognizing failures retrospectively. The authors' proposed fix is "timely, task-calibrated re-engagement," i.e. designed friction points, not better dashboards. This sharpens the standing agent-governance/oversight thread (Three-Pillar framework, RESEARCH 074 I1; Contestable Agents, RESEARCH 075 I1; Reduced-Supervision Paradox, RESEARCH 076 I1) by moving from framework-level argument to a measured interaction-level mechanism: the Reduced-Supervision Paradox paper (076) argued observability isn't the same as a real checkpoint; this paper shows concretely why a "checkpoint" fails even when a human is looking right at it — a silent defect simply looks fine. Directly relevant to this repository's own review-cockpit pattern, where a human reviews a logged card after the fact: this paper's finding — that generic review without diagnostic effort misses exactly the defects that matter — names a specific risk in that design, not a hypothetical one. Caveat: small N (36 + 12 formative), one domain (programming tasks), lab-controlled; directional, not a population estimate. Directly `WebFetch`-confirmed on the arXiv abstract page.
