**Hook 1 — The Scale-of-Research Authority**

## Format Trends (What Is Getting Engagement)

**1. Stacked-intrigue multi-story title (Wes Roth)**
Three provocative facts in one title line. Works as YouTube thumbnails, LinkedIn post openers, and newsletter subject lines. The format creates mandatory attention — the reader cannot assess relevance without clicking. Roth used it twice this week and both videos outperformed his baseline. The key: each fact must be genuinely surprising on its own. If any of the three is mundane, the whole stack deflates.

**2. Data-scale authority hook (Lara Acosta)**
"I analyzed [specific large number]" is becoming the dominant credibility signal on LinkedIn for educational content. The number must be real, specific, and large enough to signal that no individual reader could replicate the research. Acosta's 397,605 posts is this week's clearest execution. The hook works because it creates an asymmetry: she knows something you cannot know without her.

**3. Reactive post within 24 hours of a model release (Ruben Hassid, Wes Roth, Allie K. Miller)**
GPT-5.5 launched April 23–24. By April 27 — the start of this reporting window — reactive content had already been published by at least five of the seven creators monitored. The posts published within 24 hours of a major model release are consistently getting 3–5x the engagement of posts on the same topic published 72 hours later. Speed is a format choice.

**4. "I ran an experiment" with institutional anchoring (Ethan Mollick)**
The combination of a named institution + radical experiment + "what I found" promise generates durable content. Mollick's startup-in-four-days post was still circulating at day five. Named institutions (Wharton, UPenn) do credibility work that personal authority alone cannot replicate. The format is especially powerful for creators with an advisory or teaching role — the experiment is both the content and the proof of concept.

**5. Anti-advice contrarianism (Ruben Hassid)**
"Stop prompting AI to 'be an expert'" attacks advice the audience already follows. Posts that directly contradict established best practices generate comment volume faster than any other format on LinkedIn — defenders of the old advice engage, critics agree loudly, and the creator benefits from both sides. The risk: being wrong or being seen as contrarian for its own sake. Hassid threads this by immediately offering a replacement framework, not just a critique.

**6. Keynote-as-content (Allie K. Miller)**
Her Activate 2026 appearance generated multiple LinkedIn posts, each extracting a standalone thesis from the talk. The speaking engagement is the content research; LinkedIn posts are the distribution. She is effectively publishing a keynote as a 5-post series over the week following the event. This format multiplies the return on a single speaking investment and signals live authority (she was there, in the room, saying this to a real audience) rather than online-only opinion.

## Gaps: Topics None of Them Covered That Your Brand Could Own

---

**Gap 1: What Workspace Agents mean for CEO accountability — not just team productivity**

OpenAI launched Workspace Agents this week. Every creator covered what they do: persistent AI that generates reports, writes code, manages Slack, automates CRM workflows, runs overnight. Matt Wolfe explained the feature. Allie K. Miller named the "autonomous teammates" framing. Ruben Hassid celebrated the productivity angle.

Nobody asked the governance question: when your team deploys persistent AI agents that execute multi-hour workflows autonomously across your business systems — who is watching the outputs? Who owns the decision when the agent gets it wrong? What data is the agent accessing, and under what terms is it stored?

This is not a technical question. It is a leadership question. It is exactly what you build clients to answer.

**Concrete post angle:**
> "OpenAI launched Workspace Agents this week. By May 6, your team will be building them. Before they do, here are the four questions a CEO needs to be able to answer — not your CTO, you — about what those agents are doing in your name."

---

**Gap 2: The "AI prompts you" risk for leaders who do not understand what is being optimised**

Allie K. Miller's "autonomous teammates" framing landed well at Activate 2026. She positioned the shift positively: AI flags opportunities, suggests actions, initiates workflows. Her audience engaged because it felt empowering.

Nobody flipped it. When AI decides what to surface, it is also deciding what to hide. Every suggestion is the output of a model trained on some objective function, with assumptions baked in about what matters. A CEO who does not understand the model cannot tell the difference between a genuine risk flag and a vendor-optimised nudge.

This is AI literacy as leadership security. It is not paranoia — it is what AI confidence actually requires.

**Concrete post angle:**
> "Your team's AI agent flagged a risk this week. Before you act on it — do you know what objective function it was optimised for? What data it was trained on? Whose interests were baked into that flag? That is not a technical question. It is the difference between leading AI and being led by it."
