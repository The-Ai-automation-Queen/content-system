# Research Digest — 2026-06-26

This week the enterprise data and the solopreneur data finally started telling the
same story from opposite ends: the tools are working, the *systems* aren't. A
fresh batch of major surveys dropped this week confirming the gap between AI use
and AI ROI is still widening — which is the gap this brand is built to close.

## Top 5 Stories

### 1. 73% of enterprises use AI. Only 10% say it actually runs the business.
Publicis Sapient's 2026 Global Enterprise AI Report (June 17, 1,550 AI
decision-makers worldwide) put numbers to what was anecdotal: almost everyone is
"using AI," almost nobody has it embedded where it counts. 47% agree the tools are
capable enough — but 42% say their *organization* isn't set up to capture the
value. The headline: AI supports 53% of enterprise work, yet only 18% of
organizations have it fully integrated. That 63-point gap between adoption and
integration is not a technology problem. It's a workflow-design problem.
[Publicis Sapient press release](https://www.publicissapient.com/company/news/ai-adoption-enterprise-readiness-report-2026) ·
[MarketScale](https://www.marketscale.com/industries/software-and-technology/publicis-sapients-2026-enterprise-ai-report-finds-wide-adoption-but-only-10-say-its-core-to-operations)

### 2. 78% of executives can't prove their AI is working — and the ones who can are 4× more profitable
Grant Thornton's 2026 AI Impact Survey (950 senior leaders, 10 industries) landed
on a damning number: 78% of business executives lack strong confidence they could
pass an independent AI governance audit in 90 days. Grant Thornton calls it the
"AI proof gap" — organizations deploying AI can't show how decisions are made or
who's accountable. The kicker: companies with *fully integrated* AI are nearly four
times more likely to report revenue growth than those still piloting (58% vs. 15%).
Bigger budgets aren't buying better results. Governance is.
[Grant Thornton AI Impact Survey](https://www.grantthornton.com/services/advisory-services/artificial-intelligence/2026-ai-impact-survey) ·
[AI Proof Gap press release](https://www.grantthornton.com/insights/press-releases/2026/april/grant-thornton-survey-on-ai-proof-gap)

### 3. Goldman Sachs revised its job-displacement number downward — and Gen Z is still the hardest hit
Goldman's June 2026 AI Adoption Tracker updated the headline figure: AI is
displacing ~11,000 *net* US jobs per month (AI substitution wipes ~25,000/month,
augmentation adds back ~9,000). Earlier estimates ran as high as 16,000. The Gen Z
story didn't change — 13% employment decline in AI-exposed roles for workers aged
22–25. Data entry, customer service, legal support, insurance claims are the leading
categories. The counterweight: data center construction has added 212,000 jobs
since 2022 and now generates ~9,000 new positions per month — none of them the
knowledge-worker roles being displaced.
[Fortune, June 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/06/01/how-many-jobs-is-ai-destroying-goldman-sachs-11000-per-month-gen-z-economy/) ·
[Goldman Sachs full context](https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/articles/how-will-ai-affect-the-global-workforce)

### 4. EU AI Act Article 50 content-labeling deadline is now 37 days away
August 2, 2026 is the enforcement date for Article 50 of the EU AI Act: mandatory
disclosure when AI generates content (chatbots must identify as AI; deepfakes and
AI images must be labeled). Sidley Austin published a detailed compliance guide on
June 24, noting that *any* company generating AI content for European audiences is
in scope — including US-based brands posting to European users on social platforms.
Non-compliance means you're exposed even if you signed the voluntary Code of
Practice. The window for "we'll deal with it later" has closed.
[Sidley compliance guide, June 24](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2026/06/24/eu-ai-act-transparency-obligations-preparing-for-compliance-by-2-august-2026/) ·
[EU Article 50 full text](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)

### 5. One in three new startups is now founded by a single person — with AI doing the rest
The solo business economy crossed a structural threshold in 2026: 29.8 million solo
businesses in the US, contributing over $1.7T to the economy. One in three new
startups is now solo-founded (up from one in four six years ago) — driven by AI
stacks costing $75–150/month that deliver what previously required a team of 5–10.
Solopreneurs using comprehensive AI automation report saving 20+ hours per week on
average. The "you can't build a real business alone" assumption is being
empirically retired.
[AutoFaceless solopreneur statistics](https://autofaceless.ai/blog/solopreneur-statistics-2026) ·

## Signals

- **The governance gap is the next consulting category.** 78% of executives can't
  prove their AI is working. That's a massive, unfilled market for anyone who can
  help organizations *audit and prove* their AI ROI — not just implement it. This
  category doesn't have a dominant voice yet. The solopreneur angle: the same
  clarity problem exists in miniature for one-person businesses who buy tools but
  can't measure whether they're working.

  solo-founded. This isn't a "brave loner" story anymore — it's a structural shift
  in how businesses are built. Content that normalizes the solo path (and equips it)
  will land on an audience much larger than the traditional "solopreneur" niche.

- **AI model costs are becoming volatile, not stable.** Anthropic's Fable 5
  returned June 23 after the government-ordered shutdown — now at double the price
  ($10/M input, $50/M output vs. $5/$25 for Opus). AI tool budgets that felt fixed
  are now a moving target. Solopreneurs who built single-model dependencies got a
  preview of the risk. Multi-tool redundancy, not loyalty to one stack, is the
  resilient play.

## Contrarian Take

Everyone is treating Goldman Sachs' job displacement data as a call to *reskill* —
learn to prompt, learn to code, pivot into AI-adjacent work. What might be wrong:
the jobs being *created* by AI's expansion are predominantly physical infrastructure
roles (data center construction, hardware installation, electrical work) — not the
knowledge-worker positions displaced knowledge workers are pivoting toward. The
reskilling narrative assumes the same people losing AI-exposed admin and customer
service jobs will slide into AI-adjacent technical and analytical roles. But the
data doesn't show a clean swap; it shows a bifurcation. "Reskill and stay
employable" is advice designed to keep the at-risk cohort in the labor market
longer. "Build the business that runs the tools" is the advice that removes the
dependency entirely — which is an uncomfortably honest distinction nobody in the
reskilling industry has incentive to make.
