## Context: What Drove the Week

The Google I/O high wore off. This week was the reckoning.

The announcement cycle is over. No new product drops. No live keynote to react to. What remains is the harder editorial question every AI creator faces in the week after a major event: now that the excitement has subsided, what do you actually believe about what just happened?

This week separated two camps clearly for the first time.

**Camp 1: The arrival camp.** Matt Wolfe, Allie K. Miller, and Wes Roth continued building on I/O's framing — the agentic era has begun, these tools are transformative, the question is how to use them. Engagement remained high on I/O-adjacent content because search interest had not yet declined.

**Camp 2: The doubt camp.** Ethan Mollick moved in the opposite direction and generated more earned media than anyone else this week. On May 25, speaking to several hundred corporate leaders at the New York Public Library — the same week Fortune published his quote — he said: *"Nobody knows anything. We're all making this up as we go along. Anyone who says they have the playbook is lying to you."* That quote ran in Fortune, Yahoo Finance, Inkl, and Biztoc within 48 hours. It is the highest cross-platform travel of any single creator statement in six weeks of tracking this cohort.

The secondary story: the AI content labeling moment. YouTube began requiring more prominent AI-generated content labels in May 2026, with automatic detection rolling out. Simultaneously, Mollick noted this week that social media posts are "looking suspiciously similar to each other," with a growing share of comments, academic papers, and opinion articles now AI-generated. The authenticity question — who is real, what is made — is arriving faster than most creators anticipated and is not yet being addressed by any creator in this cohort as a direct business-leader concern.

The week's underlying question, which only Mollick named explicitly: *In a space where nobody actually has the playbook, what is the value of an advisor who admits it — and builds capability instead of selling certainty?*

## Top 5 Hooks Worth Adapting

---

**Hook 1 — The Epistemic Honesty Frame**
> "Nobody knows anything. Anyone who says they have the playbook is lying to you."
— Ethan Mollick, Fortune / New York Public Library (May 25, 2026)

**Why it works:** In a market saturated with AI certainty — frameworks, playbooks, guaranteed ROI — the person who says "nobody knows anything" reads as the most trustworthy voice in the room. The mechanism is counter-intuitive: admitting the limits of knowledge in a space where everyone projects confidence elevates credibility rather than destroying it. The listener who has been suspicious of AI advisor certainty feels vindicated and relieved. The hook does not say "I do not know" — it says "the people who claim to know are misleading you," which is a far stronger claim.

**Adaptation for your positioning:**
> "Most AI advisors will give you a framework, a playbook, a five-step implementation model. I have been in this space long enough to tell you something they will not: nobody has the complete playbook yet. The models change every 90 days. The use cases are still being discovered. What I can give you is something more durable than a playbook: the understanding to evaluate any framework yourself, to know when an advisor is right, and to make confident decisions even when the landscape shifts. That is AI capability. That is what stays with you when the consultant leaves."

Or the compressed LinkedIn version:
> "Any AI advisor who offers you certainty is selling you comfort, not capability. The space is moving too fast for certainty. What I build instead is judgment — the kind that holds up when the models change and the playbooks expire."

Use on: LinkedIn text post. This hook is especially powerful for your audience because it positions you as the honest alternative to the certainty-sellers without requiring you to attack them directly. The CEO who has been burned by an AI consultant who overpromised will recognise this immediately.

**Hook 2 — The Role Reversal Frame**
> "AI is starting to prompt users — not the other way around."
— Allie K. Miller, LinkedIn (May 2026)

**Why it works:** Inverts the premise of everything the reader has been learning. The reader who has invested in prompting skills encounters a frame that renders those skills secondary. The hook does not need to be explained — the implication is immediate and slightly alarming. It creates a frame shift rather than providing new information.

**Adaptation for your positioning:**
> "You have been learning how to talk to AI. Here is the conversation nobody is having yet: AI is now starting to talk to you — proactively surfacing information, prioritising your calendar, shaping your daily briefing, deciding what you see and what you do not. When an AI agent is making those decisions, you are no longer just a user. You are a subject. The CEO who understands what the agent is optimising for — who set those priorities, what the objective function rewards — is in a fundamentally different position from the one who does not. That understanding is not a technical skill. It is a leadership skill. And it is the one most AI education has not yet addressed."

Use on: LinkedIn. Best published in the wake of any major AI agent announcement — the role-reversal frame is the capability-building version of the agentic era story. Every AI creator this week covered what agents can do; this hook covers what it means that agents are now initiating.

**Hook 3 — The Superlative Provocation**
> "Prompting is the worst way to use ChatGPT."
— Ruben Hassid, LinkedIn (May 2026)

**Why it works:** Applies the superlative negative ("worst") to the most widely taught AI skill. The reader who has spent time learning prompting experiences mild anxiety. The reader who has not learned prompting feels vindicated. Both emotional states produce engagement. The hook is especially effective because it names a behaviour (prompting) that the target audience has been told to do, then tells them it is wrong.

**Adaptation for your positioning:**
> "The worst way to use AI in your business is to hand it to your team and call it an AI strategy. That is a tool subscription, not a capability. The distinction matters because a tool subscription ends when the subscription ends. A capability stays when the consultant leaves, when the model changes, when the budget is cut, when the use case evolves. Most AI implementations fail not because the tool was wrong but because the organisation never built the understanding to use it well. Stop buying tools. Start building capability."

Or the shorter version:
> "Buying an AI tool is the worst way to become an AI-capable business. It is the first step of twelve. The organisations that treat it as the last step are the ones still paying for tools three years later that nobody on their team can direct, evaluate, or improve."

Use on: LinkedIn, Instagram. The superlative provocation transfers cleanly to short-form video as a hook line. Especially effective when paired with the alternative: stop X, start Y.

**Hook 4 — The IT Department Autopsy**
> "The IT department is where AI goes to die."
— Ethan Mollick (cited in Fortune, May 25, 2026)

**Why it works:** It names a structural failure mode that every business leader suspects but has never heard articulated so precisely. The word "die" is unusual in business discourse — it is a mortality claim, not a performance claim. The mechanism: KPIs force companies into "very bad paths in the experimentation phase," which means the institutional structure that is supposed to support AI adoption is actually the primary obstacle. This is a rare case of a structural diagnosis that arrives as a single punchy sentence.

**Adaptation for your positioning:**
> "Ethan Mollick said something this week that every CEO in a mid-market business needs to hear: the IT department is where AI goes to die. The reason is not technical. It is structural. KPIs reward what is already measured. AI experimentation does not yet have a KPI. So the IT team applies the existing measurement framework to an activity that requires a different one — and the initiative stalls, fails, or quietly disappears. The answer is not to bypass IT. It is for the CEO to understand enough about AI to set the objective function before IT receives the project. That is what AI capability actually means at the leadership level."

Use on: LinkedIn thought leadership. The Mollick attribution is not necessary but strengthens the post by positioning you as someone who reads and contextualises what the most credible AI academic is saying — the same move Nina Schick uses with geopolitical data points.

**Hook 5 — The Compressed Time Claim**
> "How to build a personal brand in 90 seconds."
— Lara Acosta, LinkedIn (May 2026)

**Why it works:** Collapses an intimidating, long-horizon process into a brief learnable moment. The 90-second framing does not claim to complete the process — it claims to teach the framework for it. The distinction is small enough that most readers do not make it; they click because they want the process to be shorter than they thought. It is an accessibility hook, not an accuracy claim.

**Adaptation for your positioning:**
> "How to evaluate any AI advisor in 60 seconds: ask them one question. 'When your engagement ends, what will my team be able to do independently that they cannot do today?' If the answer is vague, they are building a dependency, not a capability. If they cannot answer it, you have your answer. AI confidence is not something an advisor gives you. Or applied to AI readiness assessment:
> "How to know if your business is actually AI-ready in 3 questions: Does your team know what data they are sharing with AI tools? Does your CEO know how to evaluate an AI output as correct or incorrect? Does your organisation have a policy for what happens when the AI is wrong? If you answered no to any of these, you are not AI-ready. You are AI-subscribed. The gap between the two is where most mid-market AI strategies currently live."

Use on: LinkedIn, Instagram. The compressed time/question-count format is the highest-portability hook structure in this cohort — it transfers across text post, carousel cover slide, and short-form video opening without adaptation.

## Format Trends (What Is Getting Engagement)

**1. Epistemic humility as the new credibility signal (Ethan Mollick)**
The biggest shift in format this week is not visual or structural — it is emotional. Mollick's "nobody knows anything" quote generated more cross-platform travel than any crafted LinkedIn post or YouTube video from this cohort. The format is spoken word, in a room, with no production — and it outperformed every produced piece of content by cross-platform citation rate. The lesson for creators: in a moment of information overload and hype saturation, the statement that admits uncertainty is the most powerful one. Authenticity has become a format advantage, not just a brand value. For any creator in the AI advisory space, the willingness to say "I do not have the complete answer" will now outperform a ten-slide framework, provided the uncertainty is accompanied by a credible diagnosis of what we do know.

**2. The role-reversal hook as the highest-portability format (Allie K. Miller)**
"AI is starting to prompt users — not the other way around" is a frame shift, not a news item. It does not depend on a specific product announcement or current event. It is applicable this week, next month, and next quarter. The role-reversal format — inverting the reader's assumed relationship to a familiar process — is the highest-portability hook structure in this cohort because it does not expire. Events-driven hooks have a 72-hour window. Frame-shift hooks have a 6-month window. The creator who consistently develops frame-shift hooks rather than reaction hooks builds a content library that compounds rather than dates.

**3. The superlative negative as peak engagement for list-format creators (Ruben Hassid)**
"Worst," "stop," "never" — applied to widely practised behaviours — consistently outperform the equivalent positive instruction on LinkedIn. "Here is a better prompting technique" generates less engagement than "prompting is the worst way to use ChatGPT." The mechanism is shame-and-curiosity in sequence: recognition (I have done this), mild embarrassment (was I wrong?), curiosity (what should I do instead?). The format is especially effective at this stage of the AI creator market because so many behaviours that were recommended 18 months ago (prompt engineering, tool-hopping, AI-first workflows) are now being revised. The creator who identifies these outdated behaviours first and names them with a superlative wins the reaction cycle.

**4. Conflict-framing outperforms information-framing on YouTube (Matt Wolfe)**
