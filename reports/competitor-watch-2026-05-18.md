## Top 5 Hooks Worth Adapting

---

**Hook 1 — The Identity Compression Provocation**
> "I give a few lines of instructions to Claude, and I am like you. You think you're too complex to fit in a text file."
— Ruben Hassid, Substack (circulating May 11–17, est. published May 3–10)

**Why it works:** The first sentence is a claim that sounds impossible. The second sentence names the objection before the reader can form it — and in naming it, partially neutralises it. The reader is now curious enough to read further. The hook does not sell the concept, it creates cognitive friction: could I really fit in a text file? The discomfort is the engagement.

**Adaptation for your positioning:**
> "Most of what your business knows exists only inside your team's heads. One resignation and it walks out the door. That is not a people problem. That is an AI capability problem — and it is the one most CEOs discover too late to fix cheaply."

Or the harder version:
> "Your senior advisor just left. Take a moment to think about everything they knew about your clients, your processes, your risk decisions. Now ask yourself: how much of that is documented anywhere? That gap has a name. It is called organisational memory. And it is your most underprotected AI asset."

Use on: LinkedIn text post. Strong bridge to your capability-building work. The hook creates urgency for a problem CEOs feel but have not yet framed as an AI question.

---

**Hook 2 — The Structural Inevitability Frame**
> "Meta and Microsoft are preparing for a combined 20,000 job cuts [...] Experts are warning this is not a temporary market correction, but a permanent structural transformation."
— Wes Roth, X/Twitter (est. May 12–14)

**Why it works:** The double-move — news + definitional reframe — is the format that travels. The news earns the read; the reframe changes the reader's category for what they are reading. "Permanent structural transformation" is not alarmism, it is a technical description. The hook removes the option of waiting this out: if the structure is changing, the strategy must change too.

**Adaptation for your positioning:**
> "92,000 tech roles have been cut in 2026. Most coverage describes this as AI replacing jobs. Here is the framing that matters more for CEOs: AI is not replacing your team. It is replacing the parts of your team that were never building your business's internal capability in the first place."

Or:
> "Your enterprise competitors are not cutting staff to save money. They are cutting staff to redeploy budget into internal AI capability. That is a different kind of threat — and it does not show up in the quarterly numbers until it is already three years ahead of you."

Use on: LinkedIn text post, Instagram reel opener. Especially effective during high-layoff news cycles. The hook reframes a news story through your positioning without chasing the story.

---

**Hook 3 — The Category Declaration**
> "We still think of AI as a chatbot that lives in the cloud. We are wrong. The future of AI is physical. Welcome to the era of Embodied AI."
— Nina Schick, LinkedIn (est. May 12–15)

**Why it works:** Three short sentences that force a category shift. Sentence one is the reader's current assumption stated as fact. Sentence two is the contradiction — delivered flat, not argued. Sentence three names the new category. The reader arrives thinking they understand AI and leaves having been told they understand the wrong version of it. The hook is a portal, not a provocation.

**Adaptation for your positioning:**
> "Most AI strategies treat AI as a software decision. Buy the right tool. Set up the right prompts. Turn it on. That is not a strategy. That is a subscription. Internal AI capability is something different — and the companies building it right now are not buying tools. They are building understanding."

Or:
> "You were told to 'adopt AI.' That advice made sense in 2023. In 2026, the companies that adopted AI are now discovering they cannot adapt it — because they never built the internal knowledge to change it when the model changes. That is the gap between AI adoption and AI confidence."

Use on: LinkedIn text post. Excellent opening for a content series on the difference between adoption and capability. The three-sentence structure (assumption → contradiction → new category) is directly transferable.

---

**Hook 4 — The Emotional Weather Report**
> "AI News: Everyone's Mad At Anthropic Now"
— Matt Wolfe, YouTube title (est. May 12–14)

**Why it works:** Names a collective emotional state rather than an event. The reader has already felt this and had no container for it — the hook provides one instantly. "Everyone's mad" is both factually approximate (not everyone, but enough) and emotionally precise. It validates a reaction before explaining a situation, which is the inverse of standard news framing. That inversion is why it performs.

**Adaptation for your positioning:**
> "Your team is exhausted by AI. Not because AI is hard. Because no one has told them what they are supposed to be doing with it — and every new tool release adds to a list of things they feel behind on. That exhaustion has a name. It is called AI confusion. And it is a leadership problem, not a technology problem."

Or the lighter version for a news-reactive post:
> "Anthropic restricted Mythos this week. Half your LinkedIn feed celebrated. The other half panicked. Neither reaction is useful — but the confusion underneath both of them is. Here is the only question a CEO needs to answer when a major AI model changes its access terms."

Use on: LinkedIn. Especially effective when the AI news cycle generates collective emotional reaction — restriction announcements, capability leaks, company controversies. The hook validates before it informs.

---

**Hook 5 — The Delegation Reframe**
> "Management as AI superpower." / "I just taught a class where students built a startup in four days. Most of them were not technical."
— Ethan Mollick, One Useful Thing / X (circulating May 13–17)

**Why it works:** Two things at once. "Management as AI superpower" is a four-word reframe of a dominant belief (prompting is the skill that matters) that travels as a standalone phrase — it does not need the rest of the post. The startup-in-four-days proof is the extreme constraint that forces the reader to update: if non-technical students can build a startup in four days with AI, what excuse remains? The hook does not explain — it demonstrates.

**Adaptation for your positioning:**
> "The CEOs I work with are not struggling with AI tools. They are struggling with AI delegation. They know how to manage people. They do not yet know how to manage AI — how to assign it work, check its outputs, and catch it when it gets things confidently wrong. That is not a technology skill. It is a leadership skill. And it is the one no one is building."

Or:
> "Two days. Twelve CEOs. None of them technical. By the end of Day 2, every one of them had identified a governance gap in their AI deployment that their IT team had missed. That is what AI confidence looks like. Not knowing the models — knowing how to interrogate what the models are doing."

Use on: LinkedIn, Instagram, keynote opening. Best used when promoting workshops or advisory offers. The extreme premise (two days, non-technical, significant result) removes the "that is not for me" objection before it forms.

## Format Trends (What Is Getting Engagement)

**1. Sentiment title over information title (Matt Wolfe)**
"Everyone's Mad At Anthropic Now" outperforms "Anthropic Restricts Mythos Access." The emotional state is the story; the information is the body. This format works on YouTube titles, LinkedIn post openers, and newsletter subject lines. The key: the sentiment must be real and collective, not manufactured. "Everyone's mad" lands because the reader already felt it. "Everyone's excited about X" rarely works by comparison — positive sentiment titles perform far below negative or frustrated ones.

**2. Structural reframe of a news event (Wes Roth)**
Take a news item (92,000 layoffs) and add a definitional frame ("permanent structural transformation, not a market correction"). The reframe is the content — the news is the excuse to post it. This format requires confidence: the creator is telling the reader how to categorise what they are seeing. Posts that hedge ("it might be structural or it might not be") perform significantly worse. The authority is in the commitment to a frame, not the hedging around it.

**3. Concept post + extraction-ready phrase (Ethan Mollick)**
"Management as AI superpower" is four words that survive without the essay. Long-form content that contains a short extractable phrase — one that can be quoted, shared, or built on by other creators — generates the longest distribution tail of any format this week. The phrase does work the post cannot. When writing any long-form piece, identify the four-word version before publishing. If you cannot find it, the essay does not yet have a thesis.

**4. Carousel format at 6–9 slides (cross-platform data, Metricool May 2026 study)**
Carousels are generating 4x the reach of video posts on LinkedIn, with an average of 1,451 impressions per carousel vs. 605 for video. Critically: ideal carousel length has dropped from 12–13 slides to 6–9 slides. Carousel fatigue is measurable — click-through rates below 35% on slide 3 trigger a visibility penalty. The format works; the packing is the failure. Each slide must earn the click to the next one, or the algorithm stops distributing after slide 3.

**5. Identity compression / "you are simpler than you think" (Ruben Hassid)**
The "I can be you / you're just a text file" concept travelled widely because it inverts an assumed complexity. Content that reduces the reader's self-model to something surprisingly small — and then offers a reason to accept that reduction — creates strong share behaviour. Readers share it because they want to test whether others feel the same discomfort. For B2B creators, the equivalent is: "Your AI strategy is not complicated. Your reluctance to commit to one is." The reduction is the hook.

**6. Physical presence as credibility signal (Allie K. Miller)**
Her Inc. article, Columbia University appearance, and NY Interschool event each generated LinkedIn posts that locate her physically in a room with a real audience. The "I was there" signal is performing better than opinion posts this week. In a content environment saturated with AI-generated analysis, physical presence — stage, classroom, fireside chat — is becoming a differentiation mechanism. For creators with speaking or advisory roles, every in-person appearance should generate at least two posts: one before (anticipation) and one after (what I found from the room).

## Gaps: Topics None of Them Covered That Your Brand Could Own

---

**Gap 1: The CEO's duty of care when AI makes decisions on their behalf — not covered by anyone**

Allie K. Miller named the shift: AI now prompts you, not the other way around. Wes Roth named the structural transformation. Nobody asked the specific governance question: when an autonomous AI agent flags a risk, files a report, or surfaces a recommendation to your board — and it is wrong — where does the accountability sit?

This is not a legal question (lawyers will sort that out eventually). It is a leadership question. The CEO who does not understand what objective function their AI was optimised for cannot tell the difference between a genuine flag and a model bias presenting as a flag. That is not AI confusion. That is liability.

Every other creator is celebrating the capability. You can own the accountability frame.

**Concrete post angle:**
> "Your AI agent surfaced a risk this week and your team acted on it. Before you move on: do you know what it was trained to look for? What it was trained to ignore? What data gave it that confidence? These are not technical questions. They are the questions every CEO will be asked when the agent gets it wrong — and they will get it wrong. AI confidence is knowing the answers before that moment arrives."

**Gap 3: The organisational memory problem that "I can be you" exposes for businesses**

Ruben Hassid's "You're just a text file" concept applies to individuals — your voice, your style, your judgment, encoded and transferred to AI. He covered it brilliantly at the personal level.

Nobody translated this to the organisational level. Every company has exactly the same problem at scale: critical institutional knowledge exists inside individual heads, is never encoded, and walks out the door when people leave. AI does not automatically solve this. But an organisation that has built internal AI capability — that has documented its own intelligence, its own decision logic, its own risk thresholds — is a fundamentally different kind of business than one that has not.

This is a named-framework opportunity: Organisational Memory as an AI Asset. Your specific audience (CEOs of $5M–$100M companies) has this problem acutely — they are too small to have a formal knowledge management function and too large to run entirely on tribal knowledge.

**Concrete post angle:**
> "Ruben Hassid published a piece this week: your entire personality can be compressed into a text file. Your AI then writes, judges, and decides more like you. It is a brilliant personal insight. Here is the version that keeps CEOs up at night: what text file does your organisation have? Not your voice — your process logic. Your client risk assessments. Your escalation decisions. Your institutional memory. Right now, most of that exists only inside your best people's heads. That is not an HR problem. That is an AI capability problem you cannot fix after they leave."
