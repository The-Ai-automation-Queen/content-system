## Top 5 Stories Worth Knowing

### 1. EU AI Act enforcement is live — and the first targets are HR, banking, and healthcare AI

On 1 September 2026, the European Commission sent formal information requests to more than 30 AI companies under the AI Act. The first wave of scheduled compliance inspections — led by France's CNIL, Germany's BfDI, and Spain's AESIA — targets three high-risk system types: automated résumé screening, algorithmic credit assessment, and AI triage tools in private clinics. Fines are active: up to €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover for non-compliance.

**Source:** [AI Regulation News September 2026 — Cubbbix](https://cubbbix.com/blog/ai-regulation-september-2026-global-update) | [EU AI Act Enforcement Has Started — Amer Mohammed](https://amermohammed.com/news/eu-ai-act-enforcement)

**Why it matters for this audience:** The sectors getting audited first are exactly where Fatiha's audience works — talent, finance, and professional services. "We'll deal with compliance later" is no longer viable for anyone operating across EU markets.

### 3. Deloitte: AI spending is up, ROI is not — and only 34% of companies are truly changing how they work

Deloitte's 2026 State of AI in the Enterprise report (survey of 1,854 executives globally) found 25% of leaders now call AI transformative — double last year's 12% — but 56% have seen neither revenue gains nor cost savings, and 42% abandoned most AI initiatives in 2025. Worker access rose 50% in the past year, yet fewer than 10% of organizations have scaled AI inside any single business function.

**Source:** [Deloitte State of AI in the Enterprise 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/what-we-do/capabilities/applied-artificial-intelligence/content/state-of-ai-in-the-enterprise.html) | [Key takeaways — Deloitte press room](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/about/press-room/state-of-ai-report-2026.html)

**Why it matters for this audience:** Most organizations are buying access, not building capability. This is the enterprise version of Fatiha's audience problem — "we have the tools, we're not sure what to do with them."

### 4. HBR: The biggest AI strategy mistake leaders are making is urgency itself

A Harvard Business Review analysis published July 2026 argued that competitive pressure is causing leaders to skip the foundational strategy work that makes AI succeed — what the authors call the "urgency trap." Separately, a near-simultaneous NBER/MIT survey of 6,000+ senior executives found roughly 90% reported no measurable improvement in productivity attributable to AI over the past three years.

**Source:** [HBR — "When Developing an AI Strategy, Beware the Urgency Trap" (July 2026)](https://hbr.org/2026/07/when-developing-an-ai-strategy-beware-the-urgency-trap) | [ISACA/MIT executive survey data](https://www.isaca.org/resources/news-and-trends/isaca-now-blog/2025/avoiding-ai-pitfalls-in-2026-lessons-learned-from-top-2025-incidents)

**Why it matters for this audience:** The pressure to move fast is the thing making leaders move badly. Slowing down to figure out what is distinctively yours — rather than copying what competitors are deploying — is the contrarian move that the evidence supports.

At the G20 ministerial meeting in Chapel Hill, North Carolina, US tech adviser Michael Kratsios publicly called on other governments to adopt the "Carolina Principles" — a light-touch approach that avoids technology-specific regulation. The EU, whose AI Act enforcement went fully live August 2026, is moving in the opposite direction. Companies operating globally now have two structurally different compliance environments with no sign of convergence.

**Source:** [Al Jazeera — US pushes looser approach, EU pushes new law (2026-09-02)](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/2/us-pushes-looser-approach-to-ai-regulation-while-eu-pushes-new-law) | [AI Regulatory Compliance in 2026 — Collibra](https://www.collibra.com/blog/ai-regulatory-compliance-in-2026-eu-ai-act-us-orders-and-state-laws-and-how-to-operationalize)

## Contrarian Take

**Everyone is saying AI ROI is hard to prove. The data says the problem isn't the AI — it's that leaders never defined what success looks like before they started.**

The Deloitte report, the HBR urgency trap piece, and the MIT/NBER executive survey all converge on the same finding: adoption is wide, measurable outcomes are scarce. The mainstream interpretation is that AI is overhyped or that the tools aren't ready. A sharper read is that organizations skipped strategic definition entirely — they deployed tools without specifying what problem the tool was solving, what change would count as evidence it worked, or who was accountable for the result. That's not an AI problem. It's a leadership problem that AI has revealed.

The executives outperforming on ROI (the 34% Deloitte calls "transformers") are not using better tools — they are doing the judgment work first. Identifying what is distinctive, deciding what to protect from automation, specifying the outcome before touching the tool. The argument is not "slow down on AI." It's "speed requires clarity about what you're speeding toward."
