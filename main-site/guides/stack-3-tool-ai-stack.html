Put AI to work

The three-tool stack I actually use

FREE SETUP · Updated 28 Jul 2026 · 5 min read

Three tools cover almost everything. Everyone selling you a fourth is selling you a fourth.

  • Best forAnyone with eleven browser tabs open and nothing finished.
  • Careful withCollecting tools instead of finishing workflows.
  • What I would doPick three. Give each one a job. Cancel the rest this week.

Why three

Everybody wants the list of fifty AI tools. You don't need fifty. You need three, and you need each of them to have a job that nothing else is doing.

I've watched a lot of people try to get out from under their own workload by buying software, and the pattern is always the same. Eleven subscriptions, nine of them barely opened, and the same amount of work still sitting there on a Sunday night. The problem was never the tools. It was that no tool had been given a job it owned end to end.

So here's the actual shape. A brain that thinks and drafts. A megaphone that gets the work out. A net that catches what comes back. Three jobs. Three tools. That's the whole system.

One. The brain

Mine is Claude, and Claude Code for the jobs with steps in them. Yours could be ChatGPT or Gemini. The brand matters far less than the habit.

What it replaces: the blank page, the "I'll write it later", and the assistant you keep meaning to hire.

This is the thinking layer. It drafts, it sorts research, and with the terminal version it runs multi-step jobs rather than answering questions one at a time. I describe the outcome once and it does the work on a schedule.

The move that changes everything: stop using it as a clever search box. Give it one repeatable task, start to finish, and let it own that task. A search box saves you seconds. An owned task gives you back an afternoon.

Two. The megaphone

Mine is Blotato. It takes the work the brain produced and pushes it out to every platform on a schedule, from one place.

What it replaces: the hour a day of copying a post into five apps, resizing it, and forgetting one.

This is the least glamorous piece and the one that most often decides whether any of this survives contact with a busy month. Making the thing was never the bottleneck. Publishing it consistently, when you're tired and behind, is the bottleneck.

Do this

Queue, don't publish. Let the tool schedule everything into a queue and keep a human release step at the end. You get the consistency without ever waking up to something you didn't mean to send.

Three. The net

Mine is GoHighLevel, which is a CRM with follow-up built in. Any decent CRM will do the same job.

What it replaces: the leads you meant to reply to, the ones sitting in a DM you never scrolled back to, and the spreadsheet you stopped updating in March.

This is the one people skip, and it's the one that costs the most to skip. The brain makes the work. The megaphone gets attention. If nothing catches that attention and follows it up, you have built a very sophisticated way of being ignored.

The brain makes it. The megaphone sends it. The net catches it. Miss one and the other two are a hobby.

The whole point

Look at those three jobs again and notice what they have in common. None of them is "be more productive". Each one is a specific piece of work leaving your plate permanently.

That's the test I would apply to any tool before you pay for it. Not "is this impressive", but "which task does this take off me completely, and would I notice if it stopped?" If you cannot answer that in one sentence, you're not buying a tool, you're buying a feeling.

The megaphoneBlotatoScheduling and publishing across platformsAny scheduler you'll actually open
The netGoHighLevelCapturing leads and following up without youAny CRM with automated follow-up

How to start, this week

Don't buy three things today. Do this instead.

  1. Open your subscriptions. Cancel anything you have not opened in thirty days. You'll find two or three, and that pays for the stack.
  2. Pick the brain first. Give it one repeating task you resent. Run it for a fortnight.
  3. Add the net second. Not the megaphone. Catching what you already have is worth more than making more of it.
  4. Add the megaphone last, once there's something worth sending consistently.

That order matters. Most people do it backwards, start with publishing, get attention they cannot catch, and conclude that none of it works.
