Is It Safe to Connect ChatGPT to My Gmail?

What it can see, and how to give it as little access as possible. Then a list of every connection you keep.

Start the guide ↓
Fatiha Chikh | The AI Automation Queen Free guide
Shift & Lead

Give AI the access it needs, and nothing more

Connecting AI to your inbox, files or calendar can save time, and it also lets the tool read your clients' emails, contracts and invoices. This guide shows you how much access each job really needs, finds what you have already connected, and ends with a list of every connection and what you decided to do with it.

What you end up with

  • The access ladder: four levels of access, and which one is enough for each job
  • Every AI connection you already have, found in your AI tools and in your Google or Microsoft account
  • A saved prompt that explains any permission screen in plain English before you click Allow
  • Your AI access list: every connection, what it can see, and whether you keep, reduce or remove it
  • Access you don't need removed, in the AI tool and in your account

You need your AI tool (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Copilot) and the login for your email account (Google or Microsoft). Every prompt below can be copied and pasted as it is; replace the parts in [square brackets].

01

Climb the access ladder Start at the bottom.

There are four ways to give AI your information. Each step up the ladder lets it see more. Most jobs only need the first or second rung.

LevelWhat it meansWhen it is enoughWhat AI can see
1. Paste one itemYou copy one email or note into the chatReplying to one email, summarising one set of notesOnly what you paste
2. Upload one fileYou attach one document to the chatChecking one proposal, summarising one contractOnly that file
3. Connect one app, read-onlyThe AI can search one app (for example Gmail or Drive) but not change anythingFinding all emails from one client, searching your filesEverything in that app
4. Full accessThe AI can read, send, edit or delete in your appsRarely needed for a small businessEverything, and it can act for you

The rule: use the lowest level that does the job. If pasting one email works, don't connect your inbox.

Before you connect anything, remember what your inbox holds: client emails, contracts, invoices, password reset links and other people's personal details. A connection gives the AI tool access to all of it, not only the email you had in mind.

On a work account (Google Workspace or Microsoft 365), your administrator may control what can be connected. If a connection is blocked, ask them before you look for a way round it.

  1. Google (Gmail, Drive, Calendar): go to your Google third-party connections page ↗. You'll see every app that can reach your Google account. Click one to see what it can access.
  2. Microsoft (Outlook, OneDrive): sign in at account.microsoft.com ↗, open Privacy, and look for the apps and services that can access your account.
  3. Write down every AI tool you find, and anything you don't recognise.

Menu names change. If you can't find a page, open the tool's official help page above, or search "apps with access to my account" on Google or Microsoft's own help pages.

03

Read the permission screen Before you click Allow.

When you connect an app, you see a permission screen listing what the tool will be able to do. These screens are hard to read. This prompt turns one into plain English.

  1. Start connecting the app, and stop when you reach the permission screen. Don't click Allow yet.
  2. Copy the text on the screen (or take a screenshot if your AI tool accepts images).
  3. Paste it into a new chat with this prompt.
I run a small business. I'm about to connect [AI tool] to [app, for example Gmail].
I want it for this job: [the job, for example "find emails from one client and summarise them"].

Here is the text from the permission screen:
[paste the permission text]

Explain it in plain English, in a table with these columns:
Permission | What it lets the tool do | Can it read? | Can it send or change? | Can it delete? | Risk for my business (low, medium, high)

Then tell me:
1. Which permissions my job actually needs
2. Whether a lower level would do the job (pasting one item, uploading one file, or read-only access)
3. One sentence: connect, connect with care, or don't connect

Use only the text I pasted. If a permission is unclear, write [CHECK] instead of guessing.

If the answer says a lower level would do the job, use that instead. If you still want to connect, click Allow and add it to your access list in the next step.

04

Build your access list Keep, reduce or remove.

Your access list is one table with every AI connection you have. It shows what each one can see and what you decided to do with it.

Help me build my AI access list.

Here are the connections I found (from my AI tools and my Google or Microsoft account):
[list each one, for example "ChatGPT connected to Gmail", "Claude connected to Google Drive", "Unknown app: [name]"]

For each one, ask me one question at a time and wait for my answer:
- What do I use it for?
- When did I last use it?

Then give me a table with these columns:
Connection | What it can see | Can it send, change or delete? | What I use it for | Last used | Decision (keep, reduce, remove) | Why

Decide like this:
- Remove: not used in the last month, or I don't know what it is
- Reduce: used, but a lower level (paste, upload, read-only) would do the job
- Keep: used often, and the access matches the job

Use only what I tell you. Write [CHECK] where you don't know what an app can see.

Copy the table into your notes or a spreadsheet and name it AI access list. Look up every [CHECK] line on the app's help page or on your Google or Microsoft connections page.

05

Remove what you don't need In two places.

Removing a connection in the AI tool doesn't always remove it from your account. Do both.

  1. In the AI tool: go back to the page from step 02, choose the connection and click Disconnect (or Remove).
  2. In your Google account: open your third-party connections page ↗, click the app, then Delete all connections (or Remove access).
  3. In your Microsoft account: on the apps and services page from step 02, choose the app and remove its access.
  4. To reduce: disconnect, then connect again and give only the permissions the job needs. If the screen gives no choice, use paste or upload instead.
  5. Update your access list with the date you removed each one.

Found an app you don't recognise? Remove its access first, then change your email password and turn on two-step sign-in if it is off.

The honest part: a connected AI tool can't tell a client's confidential email from a newsletter. It reads what the permission allows. Less access means less to go wrong. Read the access list every 3 months and remove anything you stopped using.
