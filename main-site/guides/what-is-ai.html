What AI actually is

FREE GUIDE · Updated 28 Jul 2026 · 5 min read

It's pattern-matching at enormous scale. Once you see that, most of the hype stops working on you.

  • Best forAnyone who nods along in meetings and would rather not.
  • Careful withAssuming it understands anything. It doesn't.
  • What I would doRead this once, then never feel behind in an AI conversation again.

Forget the version you have seen in films

The thing being sold to you isn't a mind. It doesn't know things, want things or understand your business. Once you have that clear, the rest of this gets much simpler, and a lot of what you're being told starts to sound different.

What it actually does: it has read an enormous quantity of text, and from that it learned which words tend to follow which other words in which situations. When you ask it something, it works out, one piece at a time, what should come next. That's genuinely most of it.

That sounds like it should produce nonsense. It doesn't, because at sufficient scale, predicting the next word well requires something that behaves a great deal like reasoning. To finish the sentence "the contract is void because the counterparty failed to" you need to have absorbed something about how contracts work. The capability is real. The understanding isn't.

How it differs from ordinary software

Ordinary software is a set of instructions somebody wrote. If a spreadsheet adds a column wrongly, a person made a mistake in the rules, and you can find the line and fix it. It does the same thing every time, which is exactly what you want from a spreadsheet.

AI wasn't given rules. It was shown examples until it worked out the patterns itself. Nobody wrote the instruction for how to summarise a contract. It inferred that from having seen a great many contracts and summaries.

Three consequences follow, and they matter more than anything else on this page.

  • It's not consistent. Ask the same question twice and you can get two different answers. Both may be fine. That's not a fault, it's how the thing works, and it means you should never use it where the output must be identical every time.
  • Nobody can fully explain a specific answer. Not you, not the company that built it. There's no line of code to point at.
  • It will be confidently wrong. Because it produces what is plausible rather than what is true, and those two things usually overlap, right up until they don't.

Do this

Next time somebody pitches you an AI product, ask one question: what happens when it gets this wrong? If they have a good answer, keep listening. If the question surprises them, you have learned what you needed to know.

It's not thinking. It's predicting. At a scale that looks a great deal like thinking.

What it can genuinely do for you today

Stripped of the marketing, the useful list is shorter than you have been told and more valuable than you think.

  • Turn a blank page into a first draft. The single biggest time saving available to most people.
  • Read something long and tell you what is in it. Contracts, reports, forty-message email threads.
  • Change the shape of information. Notes into an email, a transcript into an action list, a spreadsheet into an explanation.
  • Answer the same question a hundred times. The work that's not hard, just relentless.
  • Explain something you don't understand, at whatever level you need. Underrated, and free.

Notice what isn't on that list. It cannot decide anything for you. It cannot be accountable. It cannot know something that's true only inside your business unless you tell it.

ChatGPT and AI: how the six words fit together

Think of it like a car

This is the analogy that makes it stick, so I'll use it once and then get out of the way.

AI is transport: the whole category. Machine learning is the internal combustion engine: the method that made modern transport possible. A large language model is a particular engine design. ChatGPT is a specific car you can buy, with that engine in it. An agent is a car that drives itself somewhere. Automation is the traffic light system, which involves no engine at all and has been working since long before any of this.

Now, when someone tells you their product has AI in it, you can ask the useful question: which part?

The six words, from biggest to smallest

WordWhat it actually meansWhere you meet it
AIThe whole field. Any software doing something that used to need a person. So broad it tells you almost nothing on its own.Every pitch deck since 2023.
Machine learningSoftware that learned patterns from examples instead of being given rules. The method underneath most of this.Fraud detection, recommendations, forecasting.
Large language modelA machine learning system trained on enormous amounts of text, which predicts what comes next. The engine in the tools you use.Underneath ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and the rest.
ChatGPTOne product, from one company, built on top of a model. A brand name, not a technology.The thing everybody means when they say AI.
AgentSoftware that takes multi-step actions towards a goal instead of answering and stopping.The current favourite word on pricing pages.
AutomationIf this happens, do that. Fixed rules, no learning, completely predictable.Most of what a small business actually needs.

That last row is the one that saves people the most money, and it's the one nobody puts in a pitch. A great deal of what gets sold as AI is a rules-based workflow with a chat box bolted on the front. Rules-based workflows are excellent. They're also cheap, predictable and have existed for twenty years.

Do this

When something is pitched to you as AI, ask: is the clever part learning from data, or is it a set of rules somebody wrote? Both are fine. Only one of them justifies the price.

Half of what gets sold as AI is automation with better marketing. Automation is cheaper and more reliable.

Hearing it in a real conversation

Here's the kind of sentence you'll meet this month, translated.

What they say: "Our AI-powered platform uses advanced agentic workflows to automate your customer journey."

What that could mean: There's a language model somewhere in the product. It takes a few steps rather than one. And there's a rules-based sequence sending emails on a schedule, which is the automation part and probably the part that actually does the work.

None of that's dishonest. It's just imprecise in a direction that happens to favour the seller. Three questions cut through it:

  • Which model is underneath, and can I choose?
  • Which parts are fixed rules and which parts genuinely decide something?
  • What happens when it gets something wrong, and how would I find out?

What to check before you trust an answer

These aren't edge cases. They're the ordinary failure modes, and knowing their names means you'll spot them.

What it's calledWhat actually happensWhat you do
HallucinationIt states something false as fact, with no hedge.Open the source. Check numbers, dates and quotes yourself.
ConfabulationIt fills a gap with a plausible story rather than saying it doesn't know.Ask what it's unsure about before you ask for the answer.
SycophancyIt agrees with the way you framed the question.Ask for the strongest argument against your position.
Prompt injectionA document or webpage contains text designed to redirect it.Treat material you didn't write as untrusted.
BiasPatterns in the training data produce uneven treatment.Ask who this answer works for and who it leaves out.

Try it in the next ten minutes

Reading about this is worth about a tenth of using it. So do this today, not this week.

Open any free AI tool. Paste in the longest email thread in your inbox. Ask: what was decided here, what is still open, and what needs me to do something? Read what comes back.

Two things will happen. You'll get a genuinely useful answer in about fifteen seconds. And you'll spot at least one thing it got slightly wrong. Both of those are the lesson. That's the whole relationship in one exercise: fast, useful, and it needs you.
