AI TERMS, MADE SIMPLE

Understand the AI terms you actually need

You do not need to memorise AI jargon. You need a simple picture in your head when someone says the word. Keep this guide nearby until those pictures become automatic.

START HERE

Start with this picture

Think of AI like a very fast helper sitting at a desk. You give it instructions. It reads information. It gives you something back. Most AI terms describe one part of that simple loop.

THE WHOLE GUIDE IN ONE LINE

You give AI a prompt → it reads what it can see → it works with a model → it gives you an answer or takes an action.

THE VOCABULARY

12 AI terms in plain English

These are the terms worth being able to picture when they come up in a conversation.

Prompt

The instruction you give the AI.

Picture it

A note you hand to a helper: “Read these emails and tell me what needs my reply.”

Why it matters: clearer instructions usually give you a better result.

Token

A small piece of text the AI reads and writes.

Picture it

A sentence cut into lots of little tiles. AI counts the tiles, not whole pages.

Why it matters: more text means more tokens, and sometimes more cost.

Context window

How much information the AI can keep in front of it at one time.

Picture it

A desk. If the desk is full, you have to remove some papers before adding more.

Why it matters: if important information falls off the desk, the answer can get worse.

Hallucination

When AI gives you something false as if it were true.

Picture it

A student who does not know the answer but writes one anyway instead of saying “I don't know.”

Why it matters: confident does not mean correct.

RAG

Retrieval-augmented generation means the AI looks up information before it answers.

Picture it

Open-book exam: question → look in the right folder → answer using what you found.

Why it matters: it helps AI answer from your documents instead of guessing from general knowledge.

Fine-tuning

Extra training that changes a model so it becomes better at a narrower kind of task.

Picture it

A general cook going to pastry school to get much better at cakes.

Why it matters: it can be useful, but many businesses do not need it for everyday AI work.

AI agent

AI that can take several steps toward a goal instead of only answering once.

Picture it

Not “tell me how to book the trip.” More like “find the options, compare them, prepare the booking, then ask me before paying.”

Why it matters: agents move AI from answering to doing.

MCP

Model Context Protocol is a standard way for AI tools to connect to other software and data.

Picture it

A universal plug. Instead of making a different cable for every tool, MCP gives them a shared connection.

Why it matters: it can make it easier for AI to work with files, databases and business tools.

System prompt

Hidden or fixed instructions that tell an AI how it should behave.

Picture it

The house rules written on the wall before you enter the room.

Why it matters: two tools can use a similar model but behave differently because their rules are different.

Multimodal AI

AI that can work with more than text, such as images, audio or video.

Picture it

An assistant that can read, look, listen and sometimes watch.

Why it matters: you can show the AI a screenshot or speak to it instead of typing everything.

Open-weight model

An AI model whose trained model files are available for others to run under its licence.

Picture it

Instead of always using someone else's kitchen, you can bring the recipe and equipment into your own kitchen.

Why it matters: some companies want more control over where the AI runs and where their data goes.

Guardrails

Rules and technical limits that stop AI from doing things it should not do.

Picture it

The rail at the edge of a staircase. It does not walk for you. It helps stop a bad fall.

Why it matters: important actions should have limits, permissions and human approval.

A QUICK TEST

The three questions to ask when jargon appears

You do not need to repeat the technical word back. Ask:

  1. What does it do?
  2. What information can it see?
  3. What can go wrong?
Keep the conversation useful.

If someone can answer those three questions clearly, you understand enough to keep the conversation useful.

READ THIS FIRST

Terminology shifts over time.

Names, capabilities and common usage can change as the tools develop.
