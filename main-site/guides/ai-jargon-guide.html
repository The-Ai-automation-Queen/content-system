The AI words they keep using

Most AI jargon is one ordinary idea wearing a lab coat. Here it is, in plain English.

Updated 28 July 20265 minute read
Written by Fatiha Chikh
Best for

Sitting in a meeting and knowing exactly what is being said.

Careful with

Nodding. That is how you end up on the wrong project.

What I would do

Keep this open for a fortnight. After that, you will not need it.

The sentence this started with

Somebody said this to me, in a real meeting, with a straight face:

“We’re running a RAG pipeline with a fine-tuned model, but the context window is limiting the agent, so we’re looking at MCP to cut the token spend.”

Everyone around the table nodded. I would guess two of them followed it. That sentence contains six pieces of jargon and one actual idea. The actual idea is roughly: our thing is expensive and does not remember enough.

Here is every word in it, and six more you will meet this week. My translation job is to give you the plain version, not to make you sound clever.

The six words in that sentence

Token

In plain English

A chunk of text, roughly three quarters of a word. It is how the tools count what you send and receive.

Why it matters

It is how you are billed. Longer conversations cost more.

Context window

In plain English

Everything it can hold in mind at once: your instructions, the conversation and the files.

Why it matters

When a long session goes strange, this is usually why.

RAG

In plain English

It looks something up in your documents before answering, rather than answering from memory.

Why it matters

It is the standard way to make a tool that knows about your business.

Fine-tuning

In plain English

Extra training to make a model better at one narrow thing.

Why it matters

It is expensive and often unnecessary. A good brief solves much of what people fine-tune for.

Agent

In plain English

Software that takes multi-step actions towards a goal rather than answering once.

Why it matters

It can be useful, and it is the word most likely to be stretched in a sales pitch.

MCP

In plain English

An agreed standard for connecting AI tools to other software and data.

Why it matters

It is one reason connecting tools is getting easier and cheaper.

Try this in the room

“Can you put that in plain English?” At least one other person was waiting for someone to ask. And the person who cannot answer has told you something useful.

Six more you will hear this week

Hallucination

In plain English

It stated something false as though it were fact.

Why it matters

This is the most important failure to know about. It does not warn you.

Prompt

In plain English

What you type. The brief.

Why it matters

The difference between a useless answer and a good one is usually here.

System prompt

In plain English

Standing instructions set by the builder, which you do not see, shaping how the tool behaves.

Why it matters

It explains why the same model behaves differently in two products.

Multimodal

In plain English

It handles more than text: images, audio and sometimes video.

Why it matters

You can photograph a document instead of typing it out.

Open weights

In plain English

The model file is published, so you can download and run it on your own hardware.

Why it matters

It can be the answer when data cannot leave your building.

Guardrails

In plain English

Rules and checks meant to stop the tool doing something unsafe or off-limits.

Why it matters

Ask what they are before putting anything customer-facing on it.

Now read the sentence again

In plain English: we built something that looks up our own documents before answering, we paid to train it on our data, it cannot hold enough in mind to do the multi-step job properly, and we are hoping a standard connector will bring the bill down.

That version invites the useful questions. Why did you fine-tune before trying a better brief? Which multi-step job, specifically? What is the bill? None of those questions are available while jargon is doing its work.

What you walk away with

Not the ability to use these words. The ability to hear through them. Jargon is not usually there to deceive you; often, the speaker is repeating what they heard. But it can have the same effect because it stops you asking.

You do not need to be technical to make good decisions here. Be the person who asks: in plain English, what does this do, what does it cost, and what happens when it is wrong?
