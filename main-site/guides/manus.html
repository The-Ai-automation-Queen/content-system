Where it earns its keep

Everything else on this site answers you. Manus goes and does the thing. You describe a job, close the laptop, and come back to a finished piece of work. That's a genuinely different proposition and it's worth understanding even if you decide not to buy it.

A real example of the shape of job it suits: find every company in this city that does this thing, get me the founder's name and a contact, put it in a spreadsheet, and flag which ones have raised money in the last year. That's four hours of tedious work for a person and it's exactly what this is built for.

It gets a real browser, so it can navigate sites, fill in forms and read pages the way a person would. Underneath, several specialist agents split the work between them: one plans, one browses, one writes code, one checks the result. And you can replay what it did, step by step, which matters more than it sounds. When it gets something wrong you can see where.

Where it'll burn you

The credit system is the first thing that will catch you. You're not buying a monthly allowance of conversations, you're buying credits, and a complicated job eats them at a rate you cannot predict before you run it. People burn a month's allocation on one badly framed task in an afternoon. Set a cap before you start.

The second is that a vague brief produces a confident, thorough, expensive piece of work that answers a question you didn't ask. There's no chance to course-correct halfway, because you were not there. With a chat tool a bad prompt costs you thirty seconds. Here it costs you an hour of compute and your credits.

The third is the serious one. It can act in the world. Fill in forms, submit things, use credentials you gave it. Think carefully before you hand over a login, and never give it access to anything that can spend money. Not because it's malicious, but because it's literal, and literal plus autonomous plus your payment details is a combination you don't want to test.

Do this

Write the brief as though you were handing it to a contractor who cannot ask you questions. What the finished thing looks like, what counts as done, what to skip, and what to do when it gets stuck. If you cannot write that, the task isn't ready.

With a chat tool a bad brief costs you a minute. With an agent it costs you the afternoon and the credits.

What I'd actually do

Don't sign up for a year. Buy one month, and go in with three jobs already written down: things you have genuinely been putting off, that are tedious rather than delicate, and where a wrong answer costs you nothing.

Run all three. Read the replays. Then work out what the same three jobs would have cost you in your own hours or a freelancer's invoice, and compare. That comparison is the whole decision, and a month is enough to make it properly.

The words you'll keep hearing

  • Agent. Software that takes actions to reach a goal, rather than answering and stopping.
  • Credits. How you're billed. Longer and more complex tasks consume more, and you cannot fully predict it in advance.
  • Computer use. It drives a real browser, clicking and typing the way a person would.
  • Replay. A step-by-step record of what it did. Read this. It's how you learn to brief it better.
  • Scheduled tasks. It runs on a timer without being asked. Useful, and set the limits first.

Is it the right one for you?

Yes, if you have recurring multi-step work that's tedious rather than delicate, if you can write a proper brief, and if you can set a credit cap and stick to it.

No, if you want a daily assistant. This isn't that, and using it as one is expensive. Start with a chat tool, get comfortable briefing it well, and come back to agents when you have a specific job that's worth handing over completely.
