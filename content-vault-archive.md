## ENTRY 017 — 2026-03-26 | x-article / substack | The Early Warning System — Tech Always Shows You What Is Coming | DRAFT

**Status:** DRAFT
**Platform:** X article / Substack
**Format:** Long-form article (Dan Koe framework), ~2,500 words — Substack edition
**Topic:** The tech industry is the forecast of society — what is happening inside tech companies now signals a tsunami of job displacement across all industries
**Hook pattern:** Confession opening → named problem → counterintuitive reframe → prescribe from experience → philosophical expansion

---
### ARTICLE (FULL)

I keep having the same conversation with people outside tech. They ask me what AI will do to their jobs. I tell them to stop looking at the headlines and start looking at what is already happening inside the companies that build the tools. That is where the future shows up first. It showed up. And it is not encouraging.

I have spent twenty years inside Dell, Intel, and Microsoft. I watched cloud computing reshape IT departments three years before it reshaped accounting firms. I watched remote work become normal inside tech teams five years before a pandemic forced it on everyone else. Every pattern I have ever seen in tech eventually became the pattern everywhere.

What I am watching now is the same thing. Except this time, the pattern is not transformation.

It is elimination.

That is what I want to write about today. Not the optimistic version. Not the "AI will create more jobs than it destroys" version. The version I actually see when I am inside the rooms where these decisions get made.

My goal is not to scare you. It is to give you the same warning that tech workers got two years ago — while you still have time to do something with it.

## I — The Early Warning System

The tech industry has always functioned as an Early Warning System for the rest of the economy.

Not because tech workers are smarter. Because the tools arrive there first. The pressure to adopt arrives first. The consequences arrive first. And whatever happens to roles inside tech companies is a preview of what will happen to equivalent roles in every other industry, usually within two to four years.

I have watched this cycle play out three times in my career.

The first time was outsourcing. In the early 2000s, tech companies began moving entire departments offshore. Customer support, QA testing, back-office operations. The rest of the economy watched and said "that is a tech thing." Five years later, accounting firms, insurance companies, and banks were doing the same thing. By then, it was too late for the people who had not seen it coming.

The second time was cloud computing. Tech companies stopped building their own server rooms. They moved everything to AWS and Azure. IT departments that had been the backbone of every company began shrinking. Again, the rest of the economy said "that is a tech thing." Within a few years, the mid-market followed. Thousands of in-house IT roles quietly disappeared.

The third time was remote work. Tech companies normalised it years before the pandemic. When COVID forced every industry to go remote overnight, tech workers barely noticed. They had been doing it for years. Everyone else scrambled.

Same pattern every time. Tech goes first. Everyone else follows. And the people who get hurt the most are the ones who assumed it would not reach them.

Right now, inside the companies I know, the conversation has shifted. A year ago, it was about augmentation. How can AI help our teams work faster? How can we give everyone a copilot?

That conversation is over.

The new conversation is about headcount. How many of these roles do we still need? If one person with AI can do what three people did without it, do we keep three people? The answer, in most boardrooms, is no. And nobody is saying this publicly because the PR risk is worse than the restructuring itself.

This is not speculation. This is what I hear in rooms where the decisions are being made. The job cuts that have already hit tech — tens of thousands in the last two years — are not a correction. They are a rehearsal.

The rest of the economy just has not seen the show yet.

## II — The wave most people are not seeing

So why does the rest of the economy feel calm?

Because there is a delay built into the system. The same delay that existed with every previous technology wave.

When cloud computing eliminated the need for on-premise server teams, it took years before mid-market companies felt it. First the big tech companies moved. Then the enterprise clients followed. Then the mid-market. Then the small businesses. By the time it reached them, the original shift was already old news inside tech.

AI is following the same path. But faster. Much faster.

The reason it is faster is simple. Previous waves required infrastructure changes. You had to buy new servers, migrate systems, retrain teams on new platforms. That took time. AI requires almost nothing. A browser. A subscription. A prompt. The barrier to adoption is essentially zero, which means the barrier to displacement is also essentially zero.

A law firm does not need to build anything to start using AI for document review. It just stops hiring the junior associates who used to do it. A marketing agency does not need a new tech stack to generate first drafts at scale. It just needs fewer copywriters. A consulting firm does not need to restructure to produce slide decks faster. It needs fewer analysts.

The roles that are disappearing inside tech companies right now — junior analysts, first-draft writers, entry-level designers, basic code review, data entry, scheduling coordinators — exist in every company on earth. The same economic logic that is removing them from tech will remove them from law firms, from marketing agencies, from consulting practices, from government departments.

Most people in those roles have not felt it yet. They will.

And here is the part that makes this wave different from the previous ones.

Outsourcing replaced jobs with cheaper jobs in another country. Cloud computing replaced on-premise roles with fewer cloud management roles. Both of those shifts created new categories of work even as they eliminated old ones.

AI does not create a new category of worker. It makes existing workers more productive. That sounds positive until you realise what it means for headcount. If every person becomes two to three times more effective, you do not need two to three times more people. You need fewer.

The uncomfortable truth is not that AI will take your job. It is that AI will take the version of your job that you are currently doing. The part that is routine. The part that is repeatable. The part you could explain to someone in thirty minutes.

What remains is the part that requires judgment, taste, relationships, and the ability to know when the tool got it wrong. That is not a skill most people have been asked to develop. Because until now, the routine work was the job.

## III — What to do before the wave arrives

I am not going to tell you to "upskill" or "learn AI." That advice is everywhere and it is almost useless without context. It is the equivalent of telling someone in 2005 to "learn the internet." What does that even mean? Which part? For what purpose?

Here is what I actually tell people when they ask me. Four things. None of them are about technology.

Audit your own role honestly. Not the job description on your contract. The actual work you do every week. Write down everything — every task, every deliverable, every meeting, every report. Then mark each one with a simple question: could AI do this at 80% quality without me?

Be brutal about it. Most people overestimate their own irreplaceability because they confuse being busy with being valuable. Being busy means you have tasks. Being valuable means someone would notice a difference if you were gone. Those are not the same thing.

If more than half your weekly output could be done by AI at 80% quality, you have a problem. Not today. But within 18 months, someone above you is going to notice that too.

Stop confusing tool knowledge with value. Knowing how to use ChatGPT is not a competitive advantage. Hundreds of millions of people can use ChatGPT. Knowing when ChatGPT is wrong — and being able to prove it — is. That is a fundamentally different skill. It requires domain knowledge, critical thinking, and the willingness to challenge output that looks polished but is subtly wrong.

I run workshops where I hand executives an AI-generated strategy brief with a deliberate error. Last time, fourteen people in the room. Two found it. Not because the others were not intelligent. Because the document looked credible. Their brains stopped checking.

The people who will survive this wave are not the ones who adopt AI fastest. They are the ones who develop the judgment that AI cannot replicate. Judgment is not a tool skill. It is a thinking skill. And it takes years to build.

Get closer to the decision. The further you are from the person who decides whether your role exists, the more vulnerable you are. If your output goes through three layers of management before anyone evaluates it, you are invisible. Invisible roles are the first to be automated because nobody sees the human behind the work — they only see the output. And if the output can be replicated by a tool, the human becomes optional.

Make your thinking visible. Make your judgment visible. Make the thing that only you can do impossible to miss. Sit in the room where the decisions are made. Speak up. Show your reasoning, not just your deliverable. The people who survive restructuring are never the ones who were quietly competent in a corner. They are the ones whose absence would be immediately felt.

Build something outside the system. I do not mean quit your job. I mean develop a skill, a project, a body of work that is yours. Not your company's. Not your department's. Something that proves you can think independently, create independently, and deliver value that is not tied to a single employer's org chart.

This could be writing. Consulting on the side. Building a small tool. Teaching. Creating content. Anything that demonstrates capability beyond your current role. Because if your entire professional identity is your job title, and that title gets restructured away, you have nothing to fall back on except a resume that describes a role that no longer exists.

That is insurance no certification can provide.

## IV — The question nobody wants to ask

There is a version of this conversation that focuses on policy. Governments should regulate. Companies should retrain. Society should prepare.

I agree with all of that. And I also know that none of it will arrive in time.

The tech industry learned this lesson decades ago. Every wave — outsourcing, automation, cloud, mobile — came with promises of managed transition. Government retraining programmes. Corporate reskilling budgets. Smooth handoffs from the old economy to the new one.

The transitions were never managed. They happened. People adapted or they did not. The ones who adapted were the ones who saw it early and moved before they were forced to.

I watched colleagues at Dell lose their roles to outsourcing in the mid-2000s. The ones who had already been building skills beyond their immediate job description found new positions within months. The ones who had spent ten years perfecting a narrow function struggled for years. Same company. Same layoff. Completely different outcomes.

The difference was not talent. It was awareness. The first group had been paying attention to the Early Warning System. The second group assumed it would not reach them.

That is what the Early Warning System is for. Not to panic. Not to predict doom. But to see clearly what is coming while there is still time to prepare.

I do not enjoy writing this. I would much rather write about the incredible things AI can do and how leaders can use it to build better organisations. I do that work every day. But I would not be honest with you if I only showed you the opportunity and hid the displacement.

Both are real. Both are coming. And the people who acknowledge both are the ones who will be ready.

The question nobody wants to ask is not whether AI will change your industry. It will. The question is whether you will still be valuable when it does.

The answer to that is not about technology. It is about what you decide to become while everyone else is still debating whether this is real.

## ENTRY 015 — 2026-03-26 | x-article / substack | The Email Ceiling — Why Most People Think AI Stops at ChatGPT | DRAFT

**Status:** DRAFT
**Platform:** X article / Substack
**Format:** Long-form article (Dan Koe framework), 800-1500 words
**Topic:** The AI literacy gap — most people reduce AI to email drafting because nobody showed them what else is possible
**Hook pattern:** Confession opening → named problem → counterintuitive reframe → prescribe from experience → philosophical expansion

---
### ARTICLE (FULL)

I have lost count of how many people have told me they use AI at work. Every single time, the example is the same: "I asked ChatGPT to draft an email." That is where the conversation ends — and that is the problem.

Not because emails are unimportant. But because reducing AI to a writing assistant is like buying a commercial kitchen and only using the microwave.

The gap between what AI can do and what most people think it can do is not a knowledge gap. It is something more specific than that.

## I — The Email Ceiling

I call it The Email Ceiling.

It is the invisible barrier where someone's understanding of AI stops at the first thing that worked for them. They asked ChatGPT to rewrite an email. It was good. They felt productive. And they never went further.

The Email Ceiling has three layers.

The first is exposure. Most people were introduced to AI through ChatGPT's free tier, which is a chat interface. A chat interface looks like a place where you type questions and get answers. So that is what people do. They never see the workflows, the integrations, the agents, the analysis capabilities — because the entry point did not show them any of that.

The second is social reinforcement. When someone at the office says "I use AI," and the response is "oh yeah, I use it for emails too," the ceiling gets reinforced. Nobody in that conversation is going to say "actually, I built an automated pipeline that processes our client data and generates weekly reports." Because that sounds like bragging. Or science fiction. Or both.

The third is fear of looking foolish. Trying something beyond email drafting means experimenting. Experimenting means failing. And failing with a tool you do not fully understand, in front of colleagues who also do not fully understand it, feels dangerous. So people stay where it is safe. They stay at the email.

The Email Ceiling is not a technology problem. It is a permission problem.

## II — The real barrier is not complexity. It is imagination.

Most conversations about the AI literacy gap focus on skills. People need training. People need courses. People need to learn prompting.

That is not wrong. But it misses the deeper issue.

The real barrier is not that people cannot learn to do more with AI. It is that they cannot imagine what "more" looks like.

When I sit with a business leader and show them that AI can analyse their last 12 months of customer feedback, identify the three patterns they missed, and draft a strategy recommendation based on those patterns — in eleven minutes — the reaction is never "that is too hard for me to learn."

The reaction is always: "I did not know it could do that."

Nobody told them.

Not the vendor who sold them the licence. Not the IT team that deployed it. Not the consultant who ran the half-day workshop on "AI fundamentals" with a slide deck from 2024.

The entire AI education system is built around the tool. How to prompt. Which buttons to press. What models to choose.

Almost none of it is built around the outcome. What problems can this actually solve for you, specifically, in your role, with your data, this week?

That is the gap. Not skill. Imagination.

## III — How to break through the ceiling

The way out is not another training programme. The way out is showing people one real thing that changes how they see the tool.

I do this in every engagement now. Before I teach anything, I ask one question: "What takes you the most time every week that you wish someone else could do?"

Then I build it in front of them. Live. In their data. With their context.

Not a demo with fake data. Not a slide explaining what is theoretically possible. The actual thing, with their actual files, producing an actual result they can use tomorrow morning.

It takes about twenty minutes.

And when it works — when they see their own messy spreadsheet turned into a clean brief, or their own inbox patterns surfaced in a chart, or their own client list segmented in a way that would have taken them two days — something shifts.

They do not need motivation after that. They need the next question.

The second thing I do is remove the pressure to be technical. Most people think "doing more with AI" means learning to code. It does not. It means learning to describe what you want clearly enough that the tool can build it. That is a communication skill, not a technical one. And most senior professionals already have it.

They just do not know it transfers.

The third thing is the hardest. I ask them to do it alone, without me in the room, before our next session. One task. One experiment. No pressure to share the result. Just the experience of trying something beyond the email and seeing what happens.

Most of them come back with three experiments, not one.

## IV — The ceiling nobody talks about

There is a larger version of this problem that almost nobody is discussing.

The Email Ceiling does not just limit individuals. It limits entire organisations. When the leadership team thinks AI is "that thing that helps with emails," the company's AI strategy becomes an email strategy. The budget reflects it. The hiring reflects it. The ambition reflects it.

And when one company's ceiling is emails while another company's floor is automated operations, the distance between them grows in months, not years.

We are not in a race to adopt AI. We are in a race to understand it. And understanding does not start with a course or a certification or a prompt library.

It starts with someone sitting next to you, in your context, with your data, and showing you the thing you did not know was possible.

The people who will fall behind are not the ones who lack access to AI. Access is nearly free.

The people who will fall behind are the ones who never had someone break the ceiling for them.

Your team does not need another prompt workshop. They need someone to teach them the word "no."
