## The 5-Part Article Structure

### Part 1 — Personal Confession Opening (2-3 sentences)

Open with a real moment from your practice. Not a thesis, not a question, not a definition. A vulnerable admission that mirrors the reader's unspoken struggle.

**Pattern:** "I [verb past tense] [specific situation]. [What it revealed or how it felt]."

**Examples of strong openings:**
- "I sat in front of a blank strategy document for 40 minutes last Tuesday. The client was paying me to know what to do with AI. I did not."
- "A CEO told me last week that his team had been using AI for six months. When I asked what they had built, he could not name a single thing."
- "I ran a workshop for 12 executives on Thursday. Three of them had never opened ChatGPT. Not because they did not want to — because nobody had shown them it was safe to try."

**Rules:**
- First sentence IS the confession. No preamble.
- Must be a specific, dated event — not a generalization.
- Must feel like something a practitioner would admit, not a content hook.
- Never start with "hey," a question, or context-setting.

### Part 2 — Named Problem with a Framework Name (200-300 words)

Name the problem with a sharp, proprietary label. Give it a proper noun. Then explain it with 2-3 sub-causes or dimensions.

**Pattern:** Introduce the named concept, then unpack it with numbered sub-points or stacked single-sentence paragraphs.

**Naming conventions:**
- "The [Noun] [Noun]" — The Pilot Graveyard, The Confidence Gap, The Tool Trap
- "The [Number] [Nouns] of [Topic]" — The Three Barriers to AI Confidence
- "[Adjective] [Noun]" — Hollow Adoption, Strategic Blindness

**Rules:**
- The name must feel discovered, not manufactured.
- Immediately after naming, translate it into plain language.
- Use a physical metaphor to make the abstract tangible.
- Each sub-point gets its own short paragraph or numbered section.

### Part 3 — Agitate with a Counterintuitive Reframe (200-300 words)

Challenge what the reader assumes. Flip the conventional wisdom. This is where the anti-hype positioning lives.

**Pattern:** "Most people think [common belief]. The opposite is true. [Reframe]."

**Anti-hype reframes for AI topics:**
- "The problem is not that AI is too hard. The problem is that it is too easy to start and too easy to do wrong."
- "Companies do not need more AI tools. They need fewer tools and more understanding."
- "Speed is not the advantage everyone thinks it is. Clarity is."

**Rules:**
- One counterintuitive claim per article. Do not stack multiple reframes.
- Support the reframe with a specific example from your practice.
- Use anti-ego nudging: "Most leaders will skip this because it sounds too simple. That is exactly why their AI adoption stalls."

### Part 4 — Prescribe from Experience (300-400 words)

Provide actionable guidance grounded in what you have actually done or advised. Not tips from a blog — steps from your practice.

**Pattern:** Concrete steps, each explained with a "why" and a "what happens when you do this."

**Rules:**
- Every prescription must reference a real scenario (client, workshop, personal experience).
- Use imperative tone: "Do this. Then do this. Here is what happens."
- Include one moment of permission-giving: "It is okay if this feels uncomfortable."
- Do not use bullet points or numbered listicles. Stack single-sentence paragraphs instead.
- Alternate between short declarative sentences and longer explanatory ones.

### Part 5 — Philosophical Expansion Closing (100-200 words)

Zoom out. Connect the specific topic to a larger truth about leadership, technology, or human capability. Leave the reader in the expanded space — do not summarize.

**Pattern:** Move from the specific (what we discussed) to the universal (what this means for how we work/think/lead).

**Rules:**
- Do not summarize the article.
- Do not end with a CTA or "follow me."
- End with a standalone sentence that reframes everything.
- The last line should be quotable — something the reader would screenshot.

## Voice Rules (Non-Negotiable)

### Register
- Practitioner-grounded: teaches from experience, not theory
- Accessible-educational: makes complex ideas simple
- Strategic-executive: talks to senior leaders, not beginners
- Anti-hype: positions against AI enthusiasm without substance

### Language
- Non-contracted English always ("do not" not "don't", "cannot" not "can't")
- No profanity — use blunt truths instead ("That is not a strategy. That is a wish.")
- No exclamation marks for enthusiasm
- No banned phrases: "game-changer", "landscape", "delve", "tapestry", "in today's world", "it is important to note", "let us dive in", "navigate", "leverage", "at the end of the day", "unlock", "deep dive", "without further ado", "let that sink in", "here is the thing"

### Rhythm
- 30-40% of paragraphs are a single sentence
- Use anaphora (repeated sentence starters) at least once per article
- Use rhetorical questions as section transitions
- Alternate between short punchy declarations and longer explanatory passages
- Use physical metaphors for abstract concepts (body, food, architecture, weather — never abstract-to-abstract)

### Personality
- Blunt but warm
- Self-aware practitioner
- Anti-hype philosopher
- Reluctant authority (earned through practice, not credentials)
- Fellow traveler who happens to have a map

### What distinguishes this from Dan Koe
- No profanity (Koe uses occasional f-bombs)
- AI/technology focus instead of creativity/philosophy
- Practitioner authority from enterprise experience (Dell, Intel, Microsoft) instead of solopreneur credibility
- Senior leader audience instead of young entrepreneur audience
- Anti-hype positioning instead of anti-productivity positioning
